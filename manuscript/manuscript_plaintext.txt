# TITLE
Does Rubric-Guided Self-Refinement Improve Prompts from Small-Model Optimizers? A Scoped Negative-Result Study with Qwen3-1.7B

# AUTHORS
Linh Nguyen, Quang-Vinh Dang, Minh Ngoc Dinh, Thuy Nguyen

# KEYWORDS
Automatic prompt optimization, Big-Bench Hard, Large language models, Meta-prompting, Negative results, Prompt engineering

# ABSTRACT
Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement (MPIR) asks a language model to score an optimized prompt against a seven-criterion rubric, rewrite it, and keep the rewrite only if it beats the original on validation questions. We re-test it after an earlier evaluation was found to have a validation leak, using a repaired, fully logged loop and decision rules fixed in advance with dated amendments. The scope is narrow: Qwen3-1.7B as target, 45 base prompts from three small-model optimizers on nine Big-Bench Hard tasks, many of them weak. With the 1.7B model refining its own prompts, the original treatment changed the prompt in 1 of 80 runs (exact 95% upper bound 5.8%), but 93-95% of its candidates had lost the answer format, so this test could not tell a failed idea from a failed treatment. A format- and example-preserving re-run (V2F) followed a positive control in which the gate accepted a known-good candidate in 24 of 26 cells. Candidates were then accepted in 15 of 44 runs, but the final prompts won in 1 cell and lost in 3 under a lenient metric validated on 400 AI-labelled responses. In exploratory probes with one run per condition, a 235B refiner won in 4 of 9 cells under strict scoring, mostly format repairs for which a rubric-free rewrite obtains gains of the same kind (paired difference +2.7 points, interval [-3.5, +9.7]); an 8B refiner changed the prompt in 1 of 9 runs. Separately, one answer-format exemplar changed strict accuracy by +19 (APE) and +42 (ProTeGi) points on average, mostly through the tag convention, and identical prompts disagree on 6.5% of examples between scorings. Conclusions are limited to Qwen3 targets, nine tasks and the optimizers tested.


# 1. Introduction


Large language models (LLMs) are used for translation (Vilar et al., 2023), summarization (Goyal et al., 2022) and question answering (Kamalloo et al., 2023) (see (Qin et al., 2025) for a survey), and are being applied in domains such as law and medicine (Colombo et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). Their accuracy depends on the prompt, and small changes in phrasing or formatting can change it substantially (Errica et al., 2025; Sclar et al., 2024). Writing and tuning prompts is therefore a practical bottleneck for reliable deployment.

Two families of approaches address that bottleneck. Manual prompting applies heuristics distilled from practice, such as chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022), role assignment (Kong et al., 2024) and few-shot demonstration (Brown et al., 2020); these are interpretable but need expertise and trial and error (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023; Reynolds & McDonell, 2021). Automatic prompt optimization (APO) searches the space of prompts with the help of an LLM, using critique, mutation, evolution or optimizer-style proposal loops (Zhou et al., 2023; Pryzant et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2025); it scales, but the prompts it produces are often less structured and harder to interpret than human-written ones (Ramnath et al., 2025). A natural idea, which we call Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement (MPIR), is to add a cheap post-hoc stage after any APO run: ask an LLM to score the optimized prompt against a rubric of manual heuristics, ask it to rewrite the prompt accordingly, and keep the rewrite only if it scores better on held-out validation questions.

Whether such a layer helps matters in practice. It costs a few model calls (Section 5.11) and can be added to any APO pipeline, but if it does not help those calls are wasted, and an ungated version can make prompts worse. A clean test is not trivial: the rewrite is produced by a model that may be no more capable than the target, the gate uses a small validation set, and benchmark accuracy depends on how answers are extracted. We therefore report a test whose decision rules were fixed in advance (with dated amendments), and we treat a null result as informative.

The idea was first evaluated with GPT-4o as the refining model and GPT-3.5-turbo as the target on the tasks of Big-Bench Hard (BBH) (Suzgun et al., 2023). That evaluation reported per-task gains over PromptWizard (Agarwal et al., 2025) but a pooled gain that was not significant (Appendix D reproduces its numbers). A later audit found that its validation stage scored candidates on the optimizer's own training examples and that its loop never scored the unrefined prompt. The first evaluation was written up as a manuscript that was not externally reviewed; its numbers are exploratory and are not used as evidence here. A pilot with Qwen3-1.7B in every role then found no effect, but its seven refinement rounds were not independent attempts, and its statistics, task choice and answer-extraction harness were open to criticism. This paper replaces the pilot with a new study.

We ask four research questions. RQ1: with the refinement loop repaired so that rounds are genuinely distinct attempts, does gated MPIR improve a base optimizer's prompt over many tasks, optimizers and seeds? RQ2: is a failure to improve a property of the candidates or of the coarse validation gate? RQ3: does a more capable refining model change the answer? RQ4: does the rubric judge measure anything related to accuracy? (Cell and run are defined in Section 4.2.) The study has three steps, and the first could not answer RQ1 on its own. Step 1: with a 1.7B refiner the original treatment changed the prompt in 1 of 80 runs and won in 0 of 45 cells, but that refiner rarely produced a usable candidate and usually dropped the answer format, so the null did not separate a failed idea from a failed treatment (RQ1, RQ2). Step 2: we re-ran the grid with a treatment that keeps the format and an example, after checking that the gate accepts a known-good candidate; more candidates were accepted, but none won under a validated lenient metric. Step 3: we probed larger refiners (RQ3); strict gains appeared in several cells, most do not hold under lenient scoring, and a rubric-free rewrite reaches gains of the same kind. The rubric score is only weakly related to accuracy (RQ4). Throughout, the claims are scoped to Qwen3-1.7B, nine tasks and base prompts that are often weak, as the title says.

The contributions are:

  - A repaired, instrumented refinement loop. Rounds differ by sampling and, in the chained variant, by the history of rejected candidates; every round is logged with its outcome (fallback, echo, duplicate, validated), its validation and test scores and the judge's rubric total.
  - A multi-seed, multi-task evaluation with rules fixed in advance. 45 base prompts (three optimizers, nine tasks, up to three seeds), decision rules fixed in a time-stamped protocol, and a random, documented choice of six tasks to complement the three pilot tasks.
  - A format-preserving re-run with a positive control and a validated lenient metric. These separate 'the treatment failed' from 'the idea failed': the gate is shown to accept a known-good candidate, the main grid is re-run with a treatment that keeps the answer format, and the lenient answer parser is checked against labelled responses.
  - Diagnostics that make the result interpretable. Oracle scoring of all candidates, acceptance-rule sensitivity, a serving-noise floor and minimum detectable effect, gated and format-protected controls, strict and lenient answer extraction, a comparison with the uncorrected algorithm and a judge-validity analysis; defects of the pipeline and the serving configuration are catalogued (Appendix C).

Section 3 describes the layer under test and Section 4 the design. Section 5 follows the three steps: the scoring harness and what the 1.7B rounds were (5.1, 5.2); the repaired grid, positive control and V2F (5.3); the gate and rubric-free rewriting (5.4, 5.5); stronger refiners (5.6); serving noise and the rubric judge (5.7, 5.8); the original defect, the decision rules and cost (5.9 to 5.11). Section 6 discusses what the evidence supports and its limits.


# 2. Related Work



## 2.1. Manual Prompting Heuristics


Prompting steers an LLM through in-context learning without changing its weights (Liu et al., 2023; Brown et al., 2020), and has been described as a form of natural-language programming (Reynolds & McDonell, 2021). Non-expert authors struggle because they get no structural feedback and over-fit to the few examples they try (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023). Experienced authors rely on heuristics. Few-shot demonstrations help (Brown et al., 2020; Dong & others, 2024), though Min et al. show that format, label space and input distribution matter more than label correctness (Min et al., 2022). Chain-of-thought prompting decomposes multistep problems (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022), and the relevance of the reasoning steps to the question and their correct order matter more than the validity of each step (Wang et al., 2023). Role prompting assigns a persona to guide reasoning (Kong et al., 2024), but its benefit is inconsistent: poorly aligned personas can hurt (Kim et al., 2025), and a large study found that adding personas to system prompts does not reliably improve performance (Zheng et al., 2024). Step-back prompting abstracts key principles before solving (Zheng et al., 2024). Models attend unevenly to information in long contexts (Liu et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025), and vendor guidance reports recency effects for some models while reporting no difference for GPT-4-class models (Microsoft, 2026); whether either matters for the short prompts studied here is an empirical question rather than a premise. Self-consistency, a decoding-time heuristic, samples several reasoning paths and votes (Wang et al., 2023); it is not used here, because decoding is greedy.


## 2.2. Automatic Prompt Optimization and Meta-Prompting


APO methods generate, evaluate and select prompts iteratively (Ramnath et al., 2025). APE induces instructions from input-output demonstrations (Zhou et al., 2023); ProTeGi treats LLM critique as a textual gradient and runs a beam search (Pryzant et al., 2023); EvoPrompt applies evolutionary operators (Guo et al., 2024); OPRO conditions an LLM on a trajectory of scored prompts (Yang et al., 2024); PromptWizard combines critique-based refinement, example selection and self-generated reasoning (Agarwal et al., 2025). DSPy, TextGrad, Self-Refine and Reflexion generalize the idea of optimizing or refining text with LLM feedback (Khattab et al., 2024; Yuksekgonul et al., 2025; Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023). Meta-prompting supplies an LLM with a template that structures the critique (see (Cui et al., 2025) for a survey): PE2 uses a step-by-step diagnosis template (Ye et al., 2024), dual-phase methods add a meta-instruction-guided initialization (Yang et al., 2024), and PROPEL injects expert-derived principles as priors (Mayilvaghanan et al., 2025). Closest in spirit to MPIR, a two-stage approach for few-shot relation extraction first searches and then locally edits the winner (Chakma et al., 2026). More recent work explores evolutionary reflection (Agrawal et al., 2026), error taxonomies (Singh et al., 2026), format optimization (Liu et al., 2025), debate-based fitness (Nair et al., 2025) and RL over edits (Lin et al., 2025), and several studies target small or open-weight models: gradient-guided optimization with small models (Das et al., 2025), minimal-edit optimization for on-device models (Shim et al., 2026), and error-driven optimization of arithmetic reasoning (P\'andy et al., 2026). Others distill reasoning behaviour into prompts (Badhe & Shah, 2026), regularize prompts for token efficiency (Shah et al., 2026), question what drives optimizer gains (Zhu et al., 2026), or codify principled instructions (Bsharat et al., 2023).


## 2.3. Limits of Self-Refinement and of LLM Judges


MPIR asks a model to critique and rewrite its own prompt, which places it in the self-refinement family (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023). Evidence on intrinsic self-correction is sobering: LLMs cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without external feedback (Huang et al., 2024), and small models need strong verifiers to benefit from correction (Zhang et al., 2024). MPIR's critic is also an LLM judge, and LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Panickssery et al., 2024). Finally, benchmark accuracy depends on prompt format and on how answers are extracted (Sclar et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), which matters here because MPIR's gate and our metrics both rely on a delimiter-based extraction. These strands predict that a 1.7B model refining its own prompts may fail for reasons unrelated to the rubric, which is why our design varies the refiner, validates the judge and re-scores answers leniently.


## 2.4. Reporting Negative Results


Reproducibility programmes (Pineau et al., 2021) and guidance on reporting experimental results (Dodge et al., 2019) call for budgets, variance and full protocols. Variance across seeds and data splits can exceed the effect of a method (Bouthillier et al., 2021), significance testing in NLP needs care (Dror et al., 2018), and small test sets leave studies under-powered (Card et al., 2020). We follow this guidance by fixing decision rules in advance, reporting per-cell paired differences with intervals rather than tests that cannot be informative, and quantifying the noise floor and the minimum detectable effect.

# 3. The MPIR Layer Under Test


MPIR is a three-stage cycle applied to a prompt P0 that an upstream APO method has already produced: (1) a language model scores P0 against a seven-criteria rubric of manual prompting heuristics and recommends improvements; (2) it rewrites P0 accordingly; (3) the rewrite is scored on validation questions and kept only if it beats the incumbent (Figure 1). We describe it here as the object under test, not as a contribution.

[FIGURE: figure_3.png] Caption: Figure 1. The MPIR cycle. A prompt P0 from an APO run enters evaluation, refinement and validation for N rounds, and the best validated prompt is returned. The drawing shows the legacy loop, in which every round starts from P0; the repaired variants of Section 3.2 sample the refiner and, in the chained variants, start each round from the incumbent prompt.


## 3.1. Problem Formulation


Let (Qv, Av) = {(qi, ai)}, i = 1..M, be a validation set of M question-answer pairs and A(L, P) the accuracy of the target model L on it under prompt P. Let R(P0) be the set of candidate prompts that the refinement loop produces from P0. MPIR returns

Pfinal = argmax over P′ in {P0} ∪ R(P0) of A(L, P′),

with ties resolved in favour of P0 (a candidate must be strictly better; because validation scores are noisy, with 6.5% of examples flipping between two scorings of the same prompt (Section 5.7), a strict inequality on 25 questions is a coarse test). Including P0 in the candidate set is what makes it possible for the procedure to conclude that no refinement helps.


## 3.2. Algorithm and Variants


Algorithm 1 is the repaired loop. It scores P0 first, so the incumbent score Abest starts at A(L, P0) instead of minus infinity. In the original implementation the incumbent score started at minus infinity, so the first round's candidate was accepted whatever it scored, which is the defect Section 5.9 quantifies. Choosing among {P0} ∪ candidates by held-out score is standard in APO loops, so we regard this as an implementation defect of ours, not as a general property of the method.

[ALGORITHM: Algorithm 1. MPIR with a validation gate (repaired)]
Require: L: target LLM; L′: refining LLM; Meval, Mrefine: meta-prompts; R: rubric; P0: prompt from an APO run; (Qv, Av): validation set; N: rounds; mode in {independent, chained}; τ: sampling temperature
Ensure: Pfinal
  Pbest ← P0; Abest ← Validate(L, P0, (Qv, Av)); H ← empty list      ▷ score the baseline first
  for t = 1 to N do
      Psrc ← P0 if mode = independent else Pbest
      S ← Evaluate(Meval, L′, Psrc, R; τ)                          ▷ rubric critique
      P′ ← Refine(Mrefine, L′, Psrc, S, H; τ)                      ▷ H is empty when independent
      outcome ← Classify(P′)         ▷ fallback, echo, duplicate or validated
      if outcome = validated then
          A′ ← Validate(L, P′, (Qv, Av)); append (P′, A′) to H
          if A′ > Abest then Pbest ← P′; Abest ← A′ end if      ▷ strict: a tie keeps the incumbent
      end if
  end for
Return: Pbest

The pilot ran what we call the legacy loop: Psrc = P0 in every round, H empty and τ = 0. Because Evaluate and Refine then receive identical input in every round, the seven rounds were one request repeated, up to serving non-determinism, and 'seven rounds without acceptance' overstated the evidence: they differed only through serving non-determinism, not by design (Section 5.2 gives the number of distinct candidates). The repaired loop differs between rounds in two ways: independent mode samples at τ = 0.7 so each round is a fresh draw, and chained mode refines the current incumbent and shows the model the earlier candidates with their validation scores, as OPRO does with its trajectory (Yang et al., 2024). We study eight variants (Table 2). Two of them, V1P and V2P, are format-protected: the answer-format line is appended to every candidate, as it is for the rubric-free rewrite and for the prompts of the base optimizers. They were added after an audit found that the refinement request does not ask the refiner to keep the answer-format line, which the 1.7B refiner then dropped in most candidates (Section 5.2); they are exploratory. We added V2F, which tests a treatment that can succeed: a chained variant whose refinement request tells the refiner to keep the answer-format line and every worked example, whose loop starts from P0 extended with one bare-tag worked example of the answer format (so that the gate's baseline already carries the format and a candidate cannot win merely by restoring it), and in which any candidate that still lacks the format line or the example has them re-attached in code. V2F is run on 44 of the 45 cells with one MPIR seed (one request exceeds the context window).

## 3.3. The Seven-Criteria Rubric


The rubric was assembled from the prompting literature (Schulhoff et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024), industry guidance (Boonstra, 2025; Anthropic, 2025; OpenAI, 2025; Microsoft, 2026) and iterative experimentation on BBH-style tasks. It scores a prompt from 1 to 5 on: (1) Role Prompting, (2) Step-Back Reasoning, (3) Guided Chain of Thought, (4) Instruction and Separation, (5) Output Format with Examples, (6) Worked Reasoning in Examples and (7) Conclusion, for a total out of 35. The evidence behind the criteria is uneven. Chain-of-thought and demonstrations are well supported (Wei et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Min et al., 2022). Role prompting is the least supported, since its benefit is inconsistent (Kim et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2024). The Conclusion criterion extrapolates positional-bias results from long contexts (Liu et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025) to short prompts, and the vendor page we cite for it states that instruction placement made no difference for GPT-4-class models (Microsoft, 2026). Industry sources are practitioner guidance rather than empirical evidence; they are living documents, so the reference list gives archived copies and access dates. The rubric was refined during the first evaluation using BBH tasks, including the three Tier-1 tasks, so Tier 1 is not independent of the rubric's development; the six random Tier-2 tasks are.

The rubric text used in the pilot was internally inconsistent: it announced 'five defined criteria', asked for 'all 6 criteria' and 'Total Score: X/25' while listing seven criteria. We found this during the audit. The primary variants use a corrected text (seven criteria, X/35); variant V2L keeps the original text as a sensitivity check.


## 3.4. Evaluation, Refinement and Output Guards


The evaluation meta-prompt (Figure 2) asks the refining model to apply the rubric to the prompt and report scores, strengths, weaknesses and 7-10 suggestions. The refinement request (Figure 3) asks for a rewrite wrapped in <START> and <END>; in chained mode it also lists the earlier candidates with their validation accuracy and asks the model not to repeat them. Three output guards exist, and each outcome is logged per round. A response without delimiters is a fallback: it cannot be validated, and the pilot's code returned P0 in that case, which ties the baseline by construction. A candidate containing phrases verbatim from the evaluation template, or APE's induction template, is an echo and is rejected before validation. A candidate textually identical to the incumbent, to P0 or to an earlier candidate is a duplicate. Only the remaining candidates are validated. The refinement request of V1 and V2 does not ask the refiner to keep the answer-format line (the free-rewrite prompt does), and a candidate is scored exactly as written; this asymmetry matters for small refiners and is examined in Section 5.2.

## 3.5. The Validation Gate


A candidate is accepted only if its accuracy on the 25 validation questions strictly exceeds the incumbent's. With 25 questions accuracy moves in steps of 4 percentage points, and one question of serving noise equals a step, so acceptance is rare by design. Whether the gate or the candidates explain the absence of accepted refinements is research question RQ2, which we answer by scoring every candidate on the full test split (the oracle analysis) and by re-applying other acceptance rules offline (Section 5.4).


# 4. Experimental Design



## 4.1. Protocol Fixed in Advance, Amendments and Decision Rules


The pilot's go/no-go rule was written in the project's planning document (REBUILD.md, commit 7612aa0, 2026-09-03) before the pilot ran. That is a time-stamped plan in a version-controlled repository, not an entry in a public registry, so we say that the protocol was fixed in advance, with listed amendments, and do not call the study pre-registered or pre-specified; several pipeline fixes were also made while the pilot ran (Appendix C). The chronology is: first evaluation and audit (before September 2026); pilot, 2026-09-04 and 05; revision protocol committed 2026-10-19 (commit 8fd1397), before any revised experiment; revised experiments, 2026-10-20 and 21; further controls, audits and probes, 2026-11-02 (Appendix F, amendment 10). The decision rules below are reproduced from that protocol. Its dated amendments, which include one change of the refiners used for D2 and later exploratory additions, are listed with their dates in Appendix F.

  - D1 (RQ1). Primary outcome per cell: paired difference in test accuracy between the final MPIR prompt and P0 (10,000-resample paired bootstrap, 95% percentile interval); a cell is a win if the difference is positive and the interval excludes zero. The effect 'reproduces' if at least 15 of 45 cells are wins and the mean cell difference is at least +2.0 points; it is 'null' if at most 2 of 45 are wins and the mean lies within ±1.0 point; otherwise 'mixed'. The protocol did not say how the up to three MPIR seeds of a cell are combined: we pool them by averaging the per-example outcomes of the final prompts over the seeds before the bootstrap. The planned 135 runs (45 cells times three MPIR seeds) were cut to 80 for compute reasons, so cells have one to three runs.
  - D2 (RQ3). Capability dependence is supported if the mean cell difference with the strongest refiner exceeds that with the 1.7B refiner by at least 2.0 points and at least 3 of 9 cells are wins with that refiner. The protocol named a local 8B refiner; because the shared GPU was too slow for the extra servers, the strongest-refiner role was reassigned on 2026-10-20 (before any result with these refiners was inspected) to GPT-6 Luna, with Qwen3-235B-A22B as an intermediate rung (a local 8B refiner was added exploratorily, Section 5.6.2). Any statement about Qwen3-235B against D2 is therefore post hoc.
  - D3 (RQ2). The gate is limiting if the best oracle candidate beats P0 on test by at least 3.0 points in at least 3 of 9 cells although the gated final prompt did not.

McNemar, GEE and Wilcoxon tests on identical prompts cannot be informative, and the Wilcoxon test on three tasks cannot go below p = 0.25, so none is reported as inference. Rubric-accuracy correlations, lenient parsing, the uncorrected-algorithm comparison, the noise floor and the minimum detectable effect are descriptive.


## 4.2. Data, Splits and Task Selection


BBH has 23 tasks in 27 task files, about 6,511 examples (Suzgun et al., 2023). Each task is split three ways with random.Random(seed).sample over the original example order: 25 optimizer-training examples, 25 validation examples for the gate, and the remainder as test (200 examples for every task used here except penguins_in_a_table, which has 96). The partition is stored with the original example indices, is shared by all optimizers and conditions for a given (task, seed), and no stage touches test data during optimization or refinement. Tier 1 is the three pilot tasks (hyperbaton, ruin_names, penguins_in_a_table) at seeds 42, 43 and 44. They were chosen because the leaked first evaluation showed the largest gains there, so they are a biased sample and may be subject to winner's-curse effects. Tier 2 is six tasks drawn uniformly at random, without replacement, from the other 24 task files with random.Random(20261019): temporal_sequences, movie_recommendation, disambiguation_qa, geometric_shapes, salient_translation_error_detection and sports_understanding, at seed 42. In total there are 45 cells, each with its own base prompt P0.

  - Cell. A (task, optimizer, split seed) combination with its own split and its own base prompt P0.
  - Run. One execution of a refinement variant on a cell with one MPIR seed (the seed of the refiner's sampling); a cell has one to three runs.
  - Variant, round. A refinement procedure (V0 to V3, V1P, V2P, V2L and V2F; Table 2); one round is one evaluate-refine-validate step, with N = 7 rounds per run.
  - Split seed, MPIR seed. The seed that draws the train, validation and test split and the optimizer's own randomness (42, 43, 44), and the seed of the refiner's sampling (1 to 3).


## 4.3. Models and Serving


The target model is Qwen3-1.7B (Yang & others, 2025) with thinking disabled and greedy decoding, at most 1024 new tokens. The refining model is the same model in the main grid. In the capability experiment the refiner is Qwen3-235B-A22B (the 2507 instruct release, a mixture-of-experts model from the target's family) or GPT-6 Luna (OpenAI, 2026), both reached through the OpenRouter API on 2026-10-20 and 21 with temperature 0.7, a 3000-token limit and, for GPT-6 Luna, minimal reasoning effort. Meta-prompt calls allow 1500 (evaluation) and 3000 (refinement) new tokens. The target and the 1.7B refiner are served with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) 0.28.0 under WSL2 on one 16 GB RTX 5000 Ada GPU, bf16 weights, a maximum context of 8192 tokens (Appendix C). Greedy decoding is not bit-reproducible under continuous batching, so we measure the resulting noise (Section 5.7). The OpenRouter model slugs are qwen/qwen3-235b-a22b-2507 and openai/gpt-6-luna; calls were made on 2026-10-20 and 21 through the router, which chooses the serving provider, and the provider name and system fingerprint were not logged, so the exact backend build cannot be recovered and a re-run could differ if the router's models have changed (Section 6.3.1). The later runs (V2F, the controls and the rescoring of Section 5.7) were produced on 2026-11-02 in a single serving session, vLLM 0.28.0 with --max-num-seqs 192 and --gpu-memory-utilization 0.86; the intermediate refiner of Section 5.6.2 is Qwen3-8B-AWQ served locally.


## 4.4. Base Optimizers and Conditions


APE and ProTeGi are clean-room implementations of the published algorithms (Zhou et al., 2023; Pryzant et al., 2023), run with few_shot_count = 0, hence exemplar-free; ProTeGi scores every successor on a full minibatch instead of using the original bandit scheme. PromptWizard (Agarwal et al., 2025) is run with synthetic-example generation disabled; its three in-context examples are drawn from the 25 training examples with model-generated reasoning, and its generated expert identity is not used at evaluation. Hyperparameters are in Table 3. Many of the APE and ProTeGi base prompts are not optimized in any strong sense: for two of the three pilot tasks they are the seed instruction "Let's think step by step." plus the answer-format line, and the APE prompt for hyperbaton is its own induction template (Appendix A). PromptWizard is a reduced configuration, because synthetic-example generation was disabled. The base prompts are therefore a mix of weak and degenerate starting points, which limits what refining them can show. Only the expert few-shot condition uses the three BBH-author exemplars (Suzgun et al., 2023). Reference conditions are zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al., 2022) and expert few-shot CoT. The free-rewrite control (Appendix A.3) rewrites P0 with no rubric; V3 adds the gate.

## 4.5. Metrics


Accuracy is the share of test examples whose delimited answer equals the gold answer after removing parentheses, whitespace and case (strict extraction, as in the pilot). Because strict extraction penalizes correct reasoning that is wrapped badly, we add a lenient accuracy computed offline from the saved raw outputs: the response counts as correct if the delimited span (or, if there is none, the last 300 characters) names exactly one option and it is the gold one. Both are reported. Strict extraction remains the metric of the gate, because MPIR's gate uses it, but it largely measures formatting (Section 5.1) and the lenient metric had not been checked against labels. We therefore validated the lenient parser on a labelled sample, labelled by an AI assistant (Section 5.1.1), restated the decision rules D1 to D3 on both metrics, and report both throughout.


## 4.6. Statistical Analysis


The exact acceptance-rate bounds are Clopper-Pearson intervals; because runs of a cell share a base prompt we give them over runs and over cells (Section 5.3). Serving variability enters the intervals in two ways: the paired bootstrap resamples test examples only, so we also repeat the scoring of every prompt behind an exploratory claim in one session and report the change in each cell difference (Section 5.7).

For every cell we report the paired difference in test accuracy between the final MPIR prompt and P0 with a 95% paired-bootstrap interval over test examples. Cells with identical prompts have a difference of zero by construction, apart from serving noise. Across cells we report counts of wins, the mean difference and a hierarchical bootstrap interval that resamples tasks and then the cells within each selected task. The noise floor is the distribution of accuracy differences between repeated scorings of the same prompt, and the minimum detectable effect at 80% power and α = 0.05 follows from the paired discordance rate (Dror et al., 2018; Card et al., 2020). Intervals use the percentile bootstrap (Efron, 1979) and, for single accuracies, the Wilson interval (Wilson, 1927).


## 4.7. Harness Defects and Deviations


Running against a live model exposed defects that mocked tests could not (truncation of long responses, answer normalization, delimiter mismatches in expert exemplars, template mismatches between an optimizer's own and MPIR's evaluation harness, two echo patterns and the unscored baseline). All are described with their magnitude in Appendix C. Defects 1 to 8 were fixed before any number reported here was produced; defects 9 to 13 were found later and are handled as Appendix C describes (in particular, the format asymmetry of V1 and V2 was not fixed in those variants but addressed by the new variants V1P, V2P and V2F).

Audit of the round classification. The echo guard (five marker phrases) and the missing-delimiter rule decide 406 of the 749 V1 and V2 rounds. The guards are therefore mostly right (about 90% precision for echoes), the dominant failure is a refiner that critiques instead of rewriting, and a tolerant parser would recover only a small minority of the lost rounds.

### 5.3.1. A Format- and Example-Preserving Re-Run (V2F) and a Positive Control


Two things are needed to interpret the V2 null: a re-run of the main grid with a treatment that preserves the answer format, and evidence that the gate and the harness can accept a known-good candidate. We report the positive control first, because it says what a null result could mean.

Positive control. For each of the 45 base prompts we offered the gate a candidate known to be good under strict scoring, P0 plus the first worked example of the BBH authors' prompt (the intervention of Section 5.1), and applied the usual rule, strict improvement of the 25-question validation score over P0. The gate accepted it in 29 of 45 cells overall: 15 of 15 ProTeGi cells, 9 of 15 APE cells and 5 of 15 PromptWizard cells; the PromptWizard prompts already contain bare-tag examples, so those acceptances do not test a known-good candidate. Of the 26 APE and ProTeGi cells in which the exemplar raises strict test accuracy, the gate accepted 24, and it never accepted the exemplar where the exemplar did not help on test (0 cases). Those 26 cells are defined by the test outcome, so 24 of 26 is a check on sensitivity to a large effect, not an acceptance rate for unknown candidates. The gate and harness can therefore detect a large strict-metric effect when one is offered, and the null of V2 is not an artefact of a gate that accepts nothing.

Bare-tag control. The exemplar adds a worked chain of thought as well as the tag. Replacing it by one sentence that shows only the final-answer tag raises strict test accuracy by +19.4 points for APE and +34.2 points for ProTeGi, against +20.0 and +42.8 points for the full exemplar (the paired interval excludes zero in 10 of 15 APE and 15 of 15 ProTeGi cells for the bare tag). Most of the effect is therefore the tag convention, and the worked reasoning adds about 1 point for APE and 9 for ProTeGi. The effect is heterogeneous: it ranges from losses of up to 45 points on two APE prompts to gains of more than 70 points on ProTeGi prompts. These values were rescored in a later session; Section 5.1 reports +19.5 and +42.3 from the earlier session.

V2F. The format-preserving variant (Section 3.2, Table 2) was run with the 1.7B model as refiner and target on 44 of the 45 cells, one MPIR seed each (the 45th cell, geometric_shapes with PromptWizard, could not be run: its refinement request exceeds the 8192-token context window by one token). Its gate baseline is P0 extended with the bare-tag worked example, so a candidate cannot win merely by restoring the format, and its re-attachment step guarantees that every candidate carries the format line and the example. The final prompt differs from the baseline in 15 of 44 runs (V2: 1 of 80), an acceptance rate of 34%; 48% of the 308 rounds still ended in a fallback or echo, 92 rounds were validated and 20 of those beat the baseline on validation (60 scored below it). Even when told to keep them, the refiner's raw reply keeps the format line in 22% of its candidates and the example header in 64%, so the re-attachment step does real work. Measured against its own baseline, V2F's final prompts win in 4 cells under strict scoring (mean +0.7 points) and in 1 cell under lenient scoring, with 3 significant lenient losses (mean -0.4 points). Measured against the unextended P0, which is what a practitioner would compare with, V2F wins in 27 cells strictly (mean +22.6 points), but that difference is the exemplar, which the positive control already shows, and the lenient wins against P0 number 13. Applying decision rule D1 to V2F against its own baseline gives 'mixed' on strict scoring and 'null' on lenient scoring (the rule scaled to the number of cells run).

What this shows. Giving the 1.7B refiner a treatment that can succeed raises the acceptance rate far above that of V2 (15 of 44 runs against 1 of 80), so the V2 null was in part a property of the unprotected treatment. It does not, however, produce reliable gains: against its own baseline the accepted candidates win in 4 cells under strict scoring and in 1 under lenient scoring, with 3 significant lenient losses. One reading is that a 1.7B model refining its own prompt behaves like a noisy search on a 25-question gate; we did not test this against a random-acceptance baseline. Three limits apply. First, V2F is a hybrid treatment: it changes the gate's baseline and re-attaches the format and the example in code, and there is no rubric-free counterpart under the same protection (V3 protects the format line but not the baseline or the example), so the lenient losses cannot be attributed to the rubric. Second, V2F was run only with the 1.7B refiner, one seed and 44 cells, and its decision rule was scaled post hoc to that number of cells. Third, the positive control shows that the gate accepts a hand-built candidate, not that a refiner can produce one. Finally, ProTeGi returned the seed instruction plus the format line in 15 of its 15 cells (Appendix G), which is why its exemplar effect is the largest; for ProTeGi the experiments test the refinement of a seed prompt, not of an optimized one.

## 5.5. Free Rewriting Versus the Rubric


In the pilot, the ungated rubric-free rewrite of the PromptWizard prompt changed strict accuracy by +2.0, -19.0 and -16.7 points on hyperbaton, ruin_names and penguins_in_a_table (Tables 4 and 5); the first is 4 test items, within the serving noise of Section 5.7, and the others are large losses, so the ungated rewrite was harmful on two of three tasks. The gated rewrite (V3) accepted a rewrite in 16 of 27 runs, against 2 of 27 for the independent rubric variant and 0 of 27 for the chained one (Tier-1 runs). Part of this gap is the format asymmetry of Section 5.2, not the rewriting style. V3 has 2 winning cells (hyperbaton / APE +17.0, ruin_names / ProTeGi +2.8; a win of 3 points or less is below the 5-point minimum detectable effect of Section 5.7 and is within serving noise) and 1 cell in which the gate accepted a significantly harmful rewrite (hyperbaton / PromptWizard -9.3), for a mean strict difference of +0.7 points; under lenient extraction it has 0 wins and 2 losses (mean -1.8 points, against -0.6 for V1 and +0.0 for V2). The gate therefore does not reliably prevent harm, since 25 questions are noisy. The rubric-free rewrite has more strict wins, partly because of the format asymmetry, and is no better under lenient scoring; we can neither credit the rubric nor say that unstructured rewriting is worse.

## 5.7. Serving Noise and Minimum Detectable Effect


Each of the nine pilot base prompts was scored five times on the test and validation splits (identical prompt, greedy decoding). Test accuracy varies with a standard deviation of 1.4 points and a range of 3.3 points per prompt on average; between two scorings of the same prompt 6.5% of examples change outcome on average (up to 16.6%), and the pilot and present scorings of the same prompt differ by up to 7.0 points. This is larger than the 2% assumed earlier in the project and is the reason a 25-question validation split is noisy. For 200 test examples the minimum detectable paired effect at 80% power and α = 0.05 is 5.0 points if only serving noise separates two prompts (paired discordance 6.5%), and 6.3 to 8.9 points if two different prompts disagree on 10-20% of examples, as candidates here do. The strict gains of the strong refiner (+30, +29, +12, +11 points) are above this floor; effects of a few points are not detectable at these test sizes.

Mixed serving settings. The inference server was restarted several times during the study, with changed memory and prefix-caching settings, so the original scorings of some runs combine configurations, and the per-run configuration was not logged. An exclusion analysis is therefore not possible. We instead rescored every prompt behind an exploratory claim, the base prompts and the final prompts of the Qwen3-235B (two MPIR seeds, V2; one, V3) and GPT-6 Luna runs (45 prompts, nine cells each), twice each in a single serving session on 2026-11-02, and compared the cell differences with the original ones. The mean strict gain of Qwen3-235B V2 moves from +8.7 to +9.2 points (seed 1) and from +12.3 to +11.6 (seed 2), V3 from +6.0 to +6.3 and GPT-6 Luna V2 from +3.0 to +3.3. The number of cells with a gain of at least the 5-point minimum detectable effect is unchanged (4 and 4 for V2 seed 1; 3 and 3 for V3), and no cell difference moves by more than 4.2 points. The headline conclusions of the exploratory probes therefore do not depend on which server configuration scored them. Between two scorings in the same session, 7.1% of examples flip on average (largest 24.5%), the same order as the 6.5% measured earlier, so the gate's noise is a property of the stack and not of the restarts.

## 5.9. Effect of the Original Defect


The original loop accepted the first round's outcome whatever it scored. With the 1.7B refiner and nine cells (MPIR seed 1, independent variant, oracle-scored candidates), applying that rule gives a mean test-accuracy change of -13.6 points relative to P0, where a fallback round returns P0 (as the pilot's code did) and an echo round is accepted as a candidate: test accuracy differed from P0's in 4 of 9 cells (worse in 4, better in 0). The candidates are those of the independent variant V1, MPIR seed 1, on the nine Tier-1 seed-42 cells, scored on the full test split; the first-round candidate was validated in 5 cells, was a fallback in 2 (where the simulated legacy rule returns P0) and an echo in 2 (where it accepts the echoed prompt). This is an offline simulation: the actual legacy loop produced identical prompts in all nine pilot cells, so the figures describe what the accept-first rule would have done to these candidates, not what the pilot did. Under the literal reading of the revision protocol (group G6: the first validated candidate is accepted unconditionally) the mean change is -29.2 points. The repaired loop changes accuracy by -0.9 points on the same candidates. These figures apply to this refiner only; their counterpart for the original GPT-4o refiner is unknown, because that configuration was not rerun.

# 6. Discussion



## 6.1. What the Evidence Supports


With Qwen3-1.7B as target and refiner, the rubric-guided layer as originally specified (V2) did not improve the prompts of any of three optimizers on any of nine tasks: the final prompt equalled P0 in 79 of 80 runs (exact one-sided 95% upper bound on the acceptance rate 5.8%), and by the decision rule fixed in advance the outcome is null (Section 5.3). The treatment, not only the idea, was at fault: the refinement request did not protect the answer-format line, so 93-95% of the candidates lost it and scored zero under strict extraction, and about 60% of rounds never produced a usable candidate. We therefore re-ran the grid with a format- and example-preserving treatment (V2F, 44 cells) after a positive control showed that the gate and the harness accept a known-good candidate in 24 of 26 cells in which it helps. Acceptance rose to 15 of 44 runs, but against its own baseline the final prompts win in 4 cells under strict scoring and in 1 under lenient scoring, with 3 significant lenient losses. In this setup the treatment can succeed and still does not help. This is a result about a 1.7B model refining its own, mostly weak, prompts, and it says little about rubric-guided refinement by a capable model.

The evidence on more capable refiners is exploratory (nine cells, one or two runs per condition). Qwen3-235B produced significant strict gains in four cells in two MPIR seeds and GPT-6 Luna in one. Most of these gains repair the answer tag on base prompts that reason about as well as before; under lenient extraction, which we validated on 400 labelled responses, they reverse in the first MPIR seed and vanish in the second. A rubric-free gated rewrite by the same refiner reaches strict gains of the same kind, and the paired difference between the rubric-guided and the rubric-free variant is +2.7 points with an interval [-3.5, +9.7] that includes zero (Section 5.6.1), so the data cannot separate 'the rubric adds nothing' from 'the rubric adds a modest benefit'. The intermediate open-weight refiner (Section 5.6.2) does not change this picture. The one gain that survives both metrics, hyperbaton with PromptWizard, is a single cell on a base prompt that opens with a garbled instruction and scores near chance. Criterion-level ablations were not run and the original GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-turbo configuration was not repeated, so the first evaluation's per-task gains remain unexplained and should not be cited as evidence for MPIR.

A second lesson concerns measurement. Answer extraction decides what the gate rewards: in our runs a strict gate accepted rewrites that repaired the tag even where lenient accuracy did not improve, and exemplar-free baselines look far weaker under strict scoring than under lenient scoring. Reporting a lenient metric next to the strict one exposed both effects.


## 6.2. Practical Guidance, Scoped to What Was Tested


The following points follow from these experiments, with Qwen3 models, nine BBH tasks and the optimizers listed; they are not general rules. (i) Before adding a refinement layer, inspect how answers are extracted and check a lenient metric: one worked example of the answer format moved strict accuracy of exemplar-free prompts by +19.5 and +42.3 points (APE, ProTeGi), and +2.3 and +10.2 points under lenient extraction. (ii) Do not accept a model's rewrite of a prompt without scoring the original prompt as a baseline; in a simulation that applies the original accept-first rule to the oracle-scored candidates of the independent variant V1 (one MPIR seed, nine cells), accuracy fell by about 14 points on average, whereas the actual legacy loop produced identical prompts in all nine pilot cells. (iii) Gate on held-out data, but note that identical prompts scored twice differ on 6.5% of examples, so differences of a few points on a few hundred questions are noise. (iv) When the refining model is as small as the target, expect most rewrites to be unusable (echoes, missing delimiters, a dropped answer format) and monitor that rate; protect the answer-format line and show one worked example of the bare tag, since the line alone did not restore accuracy (Section 5.2).


## 6.3. Threats to Validity



### 6.3.1. Internal Validity


The serving stack is not deterministic (Section 5.7): repeated scoring of one prompt spans 3.3 points on average (standard deviation 1.4), which the gate and the minimum detectable effect (5.0 points) take into account but cannot remove. The server was restarted several times with changed settings, so a few interrupted runs combine scorings taken under different configurations; rescoring the prompts behind every exploratory claim in one session left the conclusions unchanged (Section 5.7). The main grid has 80 runs instead of the planned 135, oracle scoring covers MPIR seed 1 only, the strong-refiner runs use one MPIR seed (a second for one refiner), and the format-protected variants cover only part of the nine Tier-1 seed-42 cells (Section 5.2). Two audit findings, the format asymmetry of V1 and V2 and a lenient parser that rejected bare letters, were found late; the parser was corrected and the asymmetry was addressed by new variants (V1P, V2P, V2F) while the original V1 and V2 results were kept as run. The grid was re-run with the format- and example-preserving variant V2F, with one MPIR seed per cell and on 44 of 45 cells (one request exceeded the context window). The follow-up runs and the protected variants were added after results were seen. The refiner's rewrite is gated on 25 questions, so even V2F's accepted candidates may be noise; the strong-refiner models were reached through a router that does not report the serving provider, so those runs may not be reproducible; echo and fallback rounds were audited on a sample of 80 by a language-model assistant and not by human raters, and the same holds for the lenient-parser labels. The APE prompt for hyperbaton is a degenerate artifact of our implementation (Appendix A), and many APE and ProTeGi base prompts are weak, which limits what refining them can show.


### 6.3.2. External Validity


One target model family and size was used in every run. Tier 1 was chosen from a biased statistic and its tasks were also used while the rubric was developed; Tier 2 is a random draw of six tasks from 24, with one split seed. The strong-refiner experiment covers nine cells. BBH is a public benchmark, and its questions or close variants may occur in the pre-training data of Qwen3 and of the strong refiners; contamination would raise all base accuracies and shrink the room for any refinement layer to help, and we did not test for it. The base prompts are produced by re-implemented optimizers driven by a 1.7B model, and 21 of the 45 are the seed instruction plus the format line (Appendix G), so the study speaks to refining such prompts and not to refining the output of well-tuned optimizers. The original closed-model configuration, including GPT-4o as refiner, was not rerun; GPT-6 Luna is a different model and not a replication of it.


### 6.3.3. Construct and Conclusion Validity


Strict answer extraction largely measures output formatting; the lenient metric is an approximation that we validated on 400 labelled responses (precision 98.4% and recall 96.8% against labels from an AI assistant). The rubric and its judge are weakly related to accuracy (Section 5.8), no human ratings were collected, and the rubric's criteria were not ablated. The statistics are descriptive by design: with identical prompts in most cells, tests of differences would be uninformative, the nine pilot cells share three tasks and three optimizers and are not independent, and the cell-level wins of the strong refiners carry no multiplicity correction.


# 7. Conclusion


We re-tested a rubric-guided refinement layer on prompts from small-model optimizers after repairing its refinement loop and fixing the decision rules in advance, with listed amendments. Three findings stand. First, with a 1.7B model refining its own prompts the original treatment changed the prompt in one of 80 runs and won in none of 45 cells, because that refiner often dropped the answer format and rarely produced a usable candidate; this test therefore could not separate a failed idea from a failed treatment. Second, a format- and example-preserving treatment, run after a positive control showed that the gate accepts a known-good candidate in 24 of 26 cells in which it helps, had candidates accepted in 15 of 44 runs, but the final prompts won in 1 cell and lost significantly in 3 under a lenient metric that we validated on labelled responses. Third, a much larger refiner obtained strict-metric gains in several cells, but most do not hold under lenient scoring, and a rubric-free rewrite reaches gains of the same kind in some cells, so the rubric is not shown to be what helps; the comparison is exploratory and cannot exclude a modest benefit. Two measurement lessons accompany these findings: strict answer extraction accounts for most of the apparent weakness of exemplar-free baselines, and identical prompts do not score identically on this serving stack. These conclusions are limited to Qwen3-1.7B as target and as the main refiner, nine BBH tasks, and three re-implemented optimizers whose base prompts are often weak. Whether a capable refiner with a protected format gains from a rubric, and whether the first evaluation's GPT-4o results hold, remain unknown. Code, per-round logs and generations are public.

# Appendix F. Protocol and Deviations


The decision rules of Section 4.1 were fixed in REVISION_PROTOCOL.md before any revised experiment ran (commit 8fd1397, 2026-10-19). The amendments below are recorded in that file; none is an entry in a registry. Amendments 1 to 7 were made before the results of the affected experiment were inspected; amendments 8 and 9 were made after results were seen and are exploratory; V1P and V2P were stopped before all nine cells finished. Amendment 10 lists the later additions; they were decided after the main-grid results were known and are post hoc in that sense, although none was chosen after seeing its own result.

  - 1. 2026-10-20, compute. Serving stack vLLM 0.28.0 under WSL2 with measured throughput of about 1.2k tokens/s; oracle test scoring of every validated candidate for MPIR seed 1 only; one validation score per candidate in the main grid G1; V2L with one seed and no oracle scoring.
  - 2. 2026-10-20, sampling. Refiner sampling uses temperature 0.7 without top-p (the protocol text said top-p 0.95), because top-p was about three times slower on this stack.
  - 3. 2026-10-20, grouping. Tier-1 seed-42 cells are run only in the mechanism group G2, which contains the V2 runs with oracle scoring; G1 covers the other 36 cells.
  - 4. 2026-10-20, refiners for D2. Local 4B and 8B refiners were not run in the main study (an 8B refiner was added later, amendment 10); the strongest-refiner role of D2 went to GPT-6 Luna and Qwen3-235B-A22B through OpenRouter (Section 4.1).
  - 5. 2026-10-20 and 21, scale. The main grid has 80 runs instead of the planned 135; MPIR seeds beyond 1 were cut after seed 1 was complete.
  - 6. 2026-10-20, pooling. Cell-level differences pool the MPIR seeds of a cell by averaging per-example outcomes (Section 4.1).
  - 7. 2026-10-20, optimizer implementations. No change to APE, ProTeGi or PromptWizard code or hyperparameters between the pilot and the extra seeds and tasks.
  - 8. 2026-10-21, exploratory. After the Qwen3-235B refiner showed strict-metric gains, the rubric-free gated rewrite with that refiner and a second MPIR seed were added (Section 5.6.1).
  - 9. 2026-10-21, exploratory. After an audit found that V1 and V2 candidates are scored without the answer-format line, the format-protected variants V1P and V2P were added, and the lenient parser was corrected to accept everything the strict parser accepts (Section 5.2).
  - 10. 2026-11-02, additions. Added after the main-grid results: variant V2F on 44 of the 45 cells (Section 5.3.1); a positive control and a content-free bare-tag control for the gate (Section 5.3.1); rescoring of the prompts behind the exploratory claims in one serving session (Section 5.7); a labelled validation of the lenient parser (Section 5.1.1); an audit of sampled echo and fallback rounds (Section 5.2); an intermediate open-weight refiner, Qwen3-8B-AWQ, with V2 and V3 on the nine Tier-1 seed-42 cells (Section 5.6.2); a local 8B judge, which is also the intermediate refiner of Section 5.6.2 but not the refiner of the judged strong-refiner candidates (Section 5.8); exact acceptance-rate bounds and a per-variant cost table. The grid size of the original protocol (135 runs) was not restored; V2F has one MPIR seed per cell. The original decision rules D1 to D3 were not changed, and D1 is restated for V2F and on the validated lenient metric.
