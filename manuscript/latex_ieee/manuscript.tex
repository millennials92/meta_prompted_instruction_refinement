\begin{abstract}
Large language models remain critically dependent on prompt quality, and automatic prompt optimization (APO) methods often produce output lacking the structured guidance human-crafted prompts provide. This paper reports a pre-registered go/no-go pilot testing Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement (MPIR), a lightweight rubric-guided layer that refines an APO-generated prompt through meta-prompted evaluation, refinement, and empirical validation. An initial evaluation using GPT-4o to judge and refine prompts targeting GPT-3.5-turbo reported per-task gains on Big-Bench Hard (BBH) but a pooled improvement that did not reach significance, and a later audit found its validation stage had leaked training data into scoring. To isolate whether the apparent gains reflected the refinement mechanism or the meta-model's capability advantage over its target, we reran the pilot with a single small open-weight model (Qwen3-1.7B) as target, optimizer, and refinement judge simultaneously, across three APO backbones on the three BBH tasks where the original gains were largest. The effect did not reproduce: MPIR's refined prompt was byte-identical to its base optimizer's prompt in all nine tested configurations, and pooled McNemar and GEE tests returned accuracy differences of 0.0000. Tracing the cause, we identify and fix a general defect in the refinement algorithm -- it never scored the pre-refinement prompt as a baseline -- plus six further correctness bugs found live. We report this as a boundary-condition finding: a cheap, post-hoc rubric-refinement layer of this kind appears to depend on the refining model's own capability, and its benefit is smaller than the original per-task deltas suggest.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The prompt is the specification, the interface and the test fixture of an LLM-based component at once, and unlike source code it cannot be checked by a compiler. Large language models (LLMs) perform strongly on tasks such as translation \cite{vilar2023}, summarization \cite{goyal2023} and question answering \cite{kamalloo2023} (see \cite{qin2025} for a survey), yet small changes in phrasing or formatting can change their accuracy substantially \cite{errica2025,sclar2024}. Prompt design has therefore become a bottleneck for reliable deployment in domains such as law and medicine \cite{colombo2024,zhang2025}.

Two families of approaches address that bottleneck. Manual prompting applies heuristics distilled from practice, such as chain-of-thought reasoning \cite{wei2022}, role assignment \cite{kong2024} and few-shot demonstration \cite{brown2020}; these are interpretable but need expertise and trial and error \cite{zamfirescupereira2023,reynolds2021}. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) searches the space of prompts with the help of an LLM, using critique, mutation, evolution or optimizer-style proposal loops \cite{zhou2023,pryzant2023,guo2024,yang2024,agarwal2025}; it scales but may forfeit the structure that human-written prompts carry \cite{ramnath2025}. A natural idea, which we call Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement (MPIR), is to add a cheap post-hoc stage after any APO run: ask an LLM to score the optimized prompt against a rubric of manual heuristics, ask it to rewrite the prompt accordingly, and keep the rewrite only if it scores better on held-out validation questions.

This paper does not propose MPIR as a new method. It reports a pre-specified test of whether that idea works when the same small open-weight model plays every role, and what the evidence does and does not allow us to conclude. The study has a history that readers need in order to weigh it. An earlier evaluation used GPT-4o as the refining model and GPT-3.5-turbo as the target on 23 Big-Bench Hard (BBH) tasks \cite{yuksekgonul2025}. It reported per-task gains over PromptWizard \cite{agarwal2025} on a majority of tasks but a pooled gain that was not statistically significant (Appendix D reproduces those numbers). A later code audit found two defects: the validation stage scored candidate prompts on the optimizer's own training examples, and the refinement loop never scored the unrefined prompt, so a refinement was always accepted whatever it scored. A manuscript describing that evaluation was desk-rejected by another journal without external review. The numbers in it should be treated as exploratory and are not used as evidence here.

We therefore asked a narrower question that the first evaluation could not answer: are the apparent gains attributable to the rubric-guided refinement mechanism, or to the refining model being more capable than the model it refines for? A first pilot with Qwen3-1.7B in every role found the refined prompt byte-identical to the base prompt in all nine (task, optimizer) cells. Review of that pilot, however, exposed weaknesses that we take seriously: the seven refinement rounds were not independent attempts, the composition of the outcomes was not reported, the statistics were degenerate, the task choice was biased, the capability-gap explanation was untested, and the evaluation harness floored several baselines. This revision answers each of them with new experiments, specified in a version-controlled protocol before they were run (Section 4.1).

We ask four research questions. RQ1: with the refinement loop repaired so that rounds are genuinely distinct attempts, does gated MPIR improve a base optimizer's prompt over many tasks, optimizers and seeds? RQ2: is the failure to improve a property of the candidates or of the coarse validation gate? RQ3: does a more capable refining model change the answer? RQ4: does the rubric judge measure anything related to accuracy? @@SUMMARY@@

The contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A repaired and instrumented refinement loop.} Rounds differ by sampling and, in the chained variant, by the history of rejected candidates; every round is logged with an outcome category (fallback, echo-rejected, duplicate, validated), its validation and test scores, and the judge's rubric total.
\item \textbf{A pre-specified, multi-seed, multi-task evaluation.} 45 base prompts (three optimizers, nine tasks, up to three seeds), with decision rules fixed in a time-stamped protocol and a random, documented choice of six tasks to complement the three pilot tasks.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics that make a null result interpretable.} Oracle scoring of all candidates on the test split, acceptance-rule sensitivity, a serving-noise floor and minimum detectable effect, a gated free-rewrite control, an uncorrected-algorithm comparison, and a judge-validity analysis against a stronger reference judge.
\item \textbf{Harness findings.} Strict answer extraction floors some baselines; lenient re-scoring of the saved raw outputs and a catalogue of the pipeline defects, with serving configuration, are reported (Appendix C).
\end{itemize}

Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 describes the layer under test. Section 4 gives the design and statistics. Section 5 reports results, Section 6 discusses what they support and their limits, and Section 7 concludes. Code, per-example predictions and per-round logs are public.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Manual Prompting Heuristics}

Prompting steers an LLM through in-context learning without changing its weights \cite{liu2023,brown2020}, and has been described as a form of natural-language programming \cite{reynolds2021}. Non-expert authors struggle because they get no structural feedback and over-fit to the few examples they try \cite{zamfirescupereira2023}. Experienced authors rely on heuristics. Few-shot demonstrations help \cite{brown2020,dong2024}, though Min et al. show that format, label space and input distribution matter more than label correctness \cite{min2022}. Chain-of-thought prompting decomposes multistep problems \cite{wei2022,kojima2022}, and coherent, relevant reasoning steps matter more than length \cite{wang2024,wang2023}. Role prompting assigns a persona to guide reasoning \cite{kong2024}, but its benefit is inconsistent: poorly aligned personas can hurt \cite{kim2024}, and a large study found that adding personas to system prompts does not reliably improve performance \cite{zheng2024b}. Step-back prompting abstracts key principles before solving \cite{zheng2024}; a behavioural benchmark of 35 LLMs found that it fosters model-based behaviour in sequential decision tasks \cite{codaforno2024}, which is weaker support for knowledge-intensive reasoning than the original step-back evidence. Models attend unevenly to information in long contexts \cite{liu2024,yu2025}, and vendor guidance reports recency effects \cite{microsoft2025}; whether either matters for the short prompts studied here is an empirical question rather than a premise. Self-consistency, a decoding-time heuristic, samples several reasoning paths and votes \cite{wang2023b}; MPIR does not use it.

\subsection{Automatic Prompt Optimization and Meta-Prompting}

APO methods generate, evaluate and select prompts iteratively \cite{ramnath2025}. APE induces instructions from input-output demonstrations \cite{zhou2023}; ProTeGi treats LLM critique as a textual gradient and runs a beam search \cite{pryzant2023}; EvoPrompt applies evolutionary operators \cite{guo2024}; OPRO conditions an LLM on a trajectory of scored prompts \cite{yang2024}; PromptWizard combines critique-based refinement, example selection and self-generated reasoning \cite{agarwal2025}. DSPy, TextGrad, Self-Refine and Reflexion generalize the idea of optimizing or refining text with LLM feedback \cite{khattab2024,yuksekgonul2025,madaan2023,shinn2023}. Meta-prompting supplies an LLM with a template that structures the critique (see \cite{cui2025} for a survey): PE2 uses a step-by-step diagnosis template \cite{ye2024}, dual-phase methods add a meta-instruction-guided initialization \cite{yang2024b}, and PROPEL injects expert-derived principles as priors \cite{mayilvaghanan2025}. Closest in spirit to MPIR, a two-stage approach for few-shot relation extraction first searches and then locally edits the winner \cite{chakma2026}. More recent work explores evolutionary reflection \cite{agrawal2026}, error taxonomies \cite{singh2026}, format optimization \cite{liu2025}, debate-based fitness \cite{nair2025} and RL over edits \cite{lin2025}, and several studies target small or open-weight models: gradient-guided optimization with small models \cite{das2025}, minimal-edit optimization for on-device models \cite{shim2026}, and error-driven optimization of arithmetic reasoning \cite{pandy2026}. Others distill reasoning behaviour into prompts \cite{badhe2026}, regularize prompts for token efficiency \cite{shah2026}, question what drives optimizer gains \cite{zhu2025}, or codify principled instructions \cite{bsharat2023}.

\subsection{Limits of Self-Refinement and of LLM Judges}

MPIR asks a model to critique and rewrite its own prompt, which places it in the self-refinement family \cite{madaan2023,shinn2023}. Evidence on intrinsic self-correction is sobering: LLMs cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without external feedback \cite{huang2024}, and small models need strong verifiers to benefit from correction \cite{zhang2024}. MPIR's critic is also an LLM judge, and LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases \cite{zheng2023,wang2024c,panickssery2024}. Finally, benchmark accuracy depends on prompt format and on how answers are extracted \cite{sclar2024,wang2024d,zheng2024c}, which matters here because MPIR's gate and our metrics both rely on a delimiter-based extraction. These strands predict that a 1.7B model refining its own prompts may fail for reasons unrelated to the rubric, which is why our design varies the refiner, validates the judge and re-scores answers leniently.

\subsection{Reporting Negative Results}

Reproducibility programmes \cite{pineau2021} and guidance on reporting experimental results \cite{dodge2019} call for budgets, variance and full protocols. Variance across seeds and data splits can exceed the effect of a method \cite{bouthillier2021}, significance testing in NLP needs care \cite{dror2018}, and small test sets leave studies under-powered \cite{card2020}. We follow this guidance by fixing decision rules in advance, reporting per-cell paired differences with intervals rather than tests that cannot be informative, and quantifying the noise floor and the minimum detectable effect.

\section{The MPIR Layer Under Test}

MPIR is a three-stage cycle applied to a prompt P0 that an upstream APO method has already produced: (1) a language model scores P0 against a seven-criteria rubric of manual prompting heuristics and recommends improvements; (2) it rewrites P0 accordingly; (3) the rewrite is scored on validation questions and kept only if it beats the incumbent (Figure 1). We describe it here as the object under test, not as a contribution.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let (Qv, Av) = \{(qi, ai)\}, i = 1..M, be a validation set of M question-answer pairs and A(L, P) the accuracy of the target model L on it under prompt P. Let R(P0) be the set of refinements that the refining model L' produces from P0. MPIR returns

Pfinal = argmax over P' in \{P0\} $\cup$ R(P0) of A(L, P'),

with ties resolved in favour of P0 (a candidate must be strictly better). Including P0 in the candidate set is what makes it possible for the procedure to conclude that no refinement helps.

\subsection{Algorithm and Variants}

Algorithm 1 is the repaired loop. It scores P0 first, so the incumbent score Abest starts at A(L, P0) instead of minus infinity. In the original implementation the incumbent score started at minus infinity, so the first round's candidate was accepted whatever it scored, which is the defect Section 5.8 quantifies. Choosing among \{P0\} $\cup$ candidates by held-out score is standard in APO loops, so we regard this as an implementation defect of ours, not as a general property of the method.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{MPIR with a validation gate (repaired)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require L: target LLM; L': refining LLM; Meval, Mrefine: meta-prompts; R: rubric; P0: prompt from an APO run; (Qv, Av): validation set; N: rounds; mode in \{independent, chained\}; $\tau$: sampling temperature
\Ensure Pfinal
\State Pbest $\leftarrow$ P0; Abest $\leftarrow$ Validate(L, P0, (Qv, Av)); H $\leftarrow$ empty list \Comment{score the baseline first}
\For{t = 1 to N}
\State Psrc $\leftarrow$ P0 if mode = independent else Pbest
\State S $\leftarrow$ Evaluate(Meval, L', Psrc, R; $\tau$) \Comment{rubric critique}
\State P' $\leftarrow$ Refine(Mrefine, L', Psrc, S, H; $\tau$) \Comment{H is empty when independent}
\State outcome $\leftarrow$ Classify(P') \Comment{fallback, echo, duplicate or validated}
\If{outcome = validated}
\State A' $\leftarrow$ Validate(L, P', (Qv, Av)); append (P', A') to H
\State if A' > Abest then Pbest $\leftarrow$ P'; Abest $\leftarrow$ A' end if \Comment{strict: a tie keeps the incumbent}
\EndIf
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} Pbest
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The pilot ran what we call the legacy loop: Psrc = P0 in every round, H empty and $\tau$ = 0. Because Evaluate and Refine then receive identical input in every round, the seven rounds were one request repeated, up to serving non-determinism, and 'seven rounds without acceptance' meant roughly one distinct attempt per cell. The repaired loop differs between rounds in two ways: independent mode samples at $\tau$ = 0.7 so each round is a fresh draw, and chained mode refines the current incumbent and shows the model the earlier candidates with their validation scores, as OPRO does with its trajectory \cite{yang2024}. We study five variants (Table 2).

\subsection{The Seven-Criteria Rubric}

The rubric was assembled from the prompting literature \cite{schulhoff2024,sahoo2024}, industry guidance \cite{boonstra2025,anthropic2025,openai2025,microsoft2025} and iterative experimentation on BBH-style tasks. It scores a prompt from 1 to 5 on: (1) Role Prompting, (2) Step-Back Reasoning, (3) Guided Chain of Thought, (4) Instruction and Separation, (5) Output Format with Examples, (6) Worked Reasoning in Examples and (7) Conclusion, for a total out of 35. The evidence behind the criteria is uneven. Chain-of-thought and demonstrations are well supported \cite{wei2022,wang2023,min2022}. Role prompting is the least supported, since its benefit is inconsistent \cite{kim2024,zheng2024b}. The Conclusion criterion extrapolates positional-bias results from long contexts \cite{liu2024,yu2025} to short prompts, and the vendor page we cite for it states that instruction placement made no difference for GPT-4-class models \cite{microsoft2025}. Industry sources are practitioner guidance rather than empirical evidence; they are living documents, so the reference list gives archived copies and access dates.

The rubric text used in the pilot was internally inconsistent: it announced 'five defined criteria', asked for 'all 6 criteria' and 'Total Score: X/25' while listing seven criteria. We found this during the audit. The primary variants use a corrected text (seven criteria, X/35); variant V2L keeps the original text as a sensitivity check.

\subsection{Evaluation, Refinement and Output Guards}

The evaluation meta-prompt (Figure 2) asks the refining model to apply the rubric to the prompt and report scores, strengths, weaknesses and 7-10 suggestions. The refinement request (Figure 3) asks for a rewrite wrapped in <START> and <END>; in chained mode it also lists the earlier candidates with their validation accuracy and asks the model not to repeat them. Three output guards exist, and each outcome is logged per round. A response without delimiters is a fallback: it cannot be validated, and the pilot's code returned P0 in that case, which ties the baseline by construction. A candidate containing phrases verbatim from the evaluation template, or APE's induction template, is an echo and is rejected before validation. A candidate textually identical to the incumbent, to P0 or to an earlier candidate is a duplicate. Only the remaining candidates are validated.

\subsection{The Validation Gate}

A candidate is accepted only if its accuracy on the 25 validation questions strictly exceeds the incumbent's. With 25 questions accuracy moves in steps of 4 percentage points, and one question of serving noise equals a step, so acceptance is rare by design. Whether the gate or the candidates explain the absence of accepted refinements is research question RQ2, which we answer by scoring every candidate on the full test split (the oracle analysis) and by re-applying other acceptance rules offline (Section 5.4).

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Pre-Specification and Decision Rules}

The first pilot's go/no-go rule was written in the project's planning document (REBUILD.md, commit 7612aa0, 2026-09-03) before the pilot ran. It was a version-controlled plan, not an entry in a public registry, and the pilot was not pre-registered in the strict sense in which we used that word in the first submission. Several pipeline fixes were also made while the pilot was running (Appendix C). For this revision we wrote the protocol in REVISION\_\allowbreak{}PROTOCOL.md and committed it (commit 8fd1397, 2026-10-19) before running any revised experiment; its commit time-stamps the rules below, which are reproduced from it. Dated amendments are listed in Appendix F. This is also not a registry entry.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{D1 (RQ1).} Primary outcome per cell: paired difference in test accuracy between the final MPIR prompt and P0 (10,000-resample paired bootstrap, 95\% percentile interval); a cell is a win if the difference is positive and the interval excludes zero. The effect 'reproduces' if at least 15 of 45 cells are wins and the mean cell difference is at least +2.0 points; it is 'null' if at most 2 of 45 are wins and the mean lies within $\pm$1.0 point; otherwise 'mixed'.
\item \textbf{D2 (RQ3).} Capability dependence is supported if the mean cell difference with the 8B refiner exceeds that with the 1.7B refiner by at least 2.0 points and at least 3 of 9 cells are wins with the 8B refiner.
\item \textbf{D3 (RQ2).} The gate is limiting if the best oracle candidate beats P0 on test by at least 3.0 points in at least 3 of 9 cells although the gated final prompt did not.
\end{itemize}

McNemar, GEE and Wilcoxon tests on identical prompts cannot be informative, and the Wilcoxon test on three tasks cannot go below p = 0.25, so none is reported as inference. Rubric-accuracy correlations, lenient parsing, the uncorrected-algorithm comparison, the noise floor and the minimum detectable effect are descriptive.

\subsection{Data, Splits and Task Selection}

BBH has 23 tasks in 27 task files, about 6,511 examples \cite{yuksekgonul2025}. Each task is split three ways with random.Random(seed).sample over the original example order: 25 optimizer-training examples, 25 validation examples for the gate, and the remainder as test (200 examples for every task used here except penguins\_\allowbreak{}in\_\allowbreak{}a\_\allowbreak{}table, which has 96). The partition is stored with the original example indices, is shared by all optimizers and conditions for a given (task, seed), and no stage touches test data during optimization or refinement. Tier 1 is the three pilot tasks (hyperbaton, ruin\_\allowbreak{}names, penguins\_\allowbreak{}in\_\allowbreak{}a\_\allowbreak{}table) at seeds 42, 43 and 44. They were chosen because the leaked first evaluation showed the largest gains there, so they are a biased sample and may be subject to winner's-curse effects. Tier 2 is six tasks drawn uniformly at random, without replacement, from the other 24 task files with random.Random(20261019): temporal\_\allowbreak{}sequences, movie\_\allowbreak{}recommendation, disambiguation\_\allowbreak{}qa, geometric\_\allowbreak{}shapes, salient\_\allowbreak{}translation\_\allowbreak{}error\_\allowbreak{}detection and sports\_\allowbreak{}understanding, at seed 42. In total there are 45 cells, one per (task, optimizer, seed), each with its own P0.

\subsection{Models and Serving}

The target model is Qwen3-1.7B \cite{qwenteam2025} with thinking disabled and greedy decoding, at most 1024 new tokens. The refining model is the same model in the main grid and Qwen3-4B-AWQ or Qwen3-8B-AWQ in the capability experiment; meta-prompt calls allow 1500 (evaluation) and 3000 (refinement) new tokens. Refiner sampling uses temperature 0.7. Everything is served with vLLM 0.28.0 under WSL2 on one 16 GB RTX 5000 Ada GPU, bf16 weights for the 1.7B model, a maximum context of 8192 tokens (Appendix C). Greedy decoding is not bit-reproducible under continuous batching, so we measure the resulting noise (Section 5.7).

\subsection{Base Optimizers and Conditions}

APE and ProTeGi are clean-room implementations of the published algorithms \cite{zhou2023,pryzant2023}, run with few\_\allowbreak{}shot\_\allowbreak{}count = 0, hence exemplar-free; ProTeGi scores every successor on a full minibatch instead of using the original bandit scheme. PromptWizard \cite{agarwal2025} is run with synthetic-example generation disabled; its three in-context examples are drawn from the 25 training examples with model-generated reasoning, and its generated expert identity is not used at evaluation. Hyperparameters are in Table 3. Only the expert few-shot condition uses the three BBH-author exemplars \cite{yuksekgonul2025}; the first manuscript's statement that APE and ProTeGi also used them was wrong. Reference conditions are zero-shot CoT \cite{kojima2022} and expert few-shot CoT. The free-rewrite control (Appendix A.3) rewrites P0 with no rubric; V3 adds the gate.

\subsection{Metrics}

Accuracy is the share of test examples whose delimited answer equals the gold answer after removing parentheses, whitespace and case (strict extraction, as in the pilot). Because strict extraction penalizes correct reasoning that is wrapped badly, we add a lenient accuracy computed offline from the saved raw outputs: the response counts as correct if the delimited span (or, if there is none, the last 300 characters) names exactly one option and it is the gold one. Both are reported; strict extraction remains primary because MPIR's gate uses it.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

For every cell we report the paired difference in test accuracy between the final MPIR prompt and P0 with a 95\% paired-bootstrap interval over test examples. Cells with identical prompts have a difference of zero by construction, apart from serving noise. Across cells we report counts of wins, the mean difference and a hierarchical bootstrap interval that resamples tasks, then optimizers and seeds within tasks. The noise floor is the distribution of accuracy differences between repeated scorings of the same prompt, and the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power and $\alpha$ = 0.05 follows from the paired discordance rate \cite{dror2018,card2020}.

\subsection{Harness Defects and Deviations}

Running against a live model exposed defects that mocked tests could not (truncation of long responses, answer normalization, delimiter mismatches in expert exemplars, template mismatches between an optimizer's own and MPIR's evaluation harness, two echo patterns and the unscored baseline). All are described with their magnitude in Appendix C and were fixed before the numbers reported here were produced.
