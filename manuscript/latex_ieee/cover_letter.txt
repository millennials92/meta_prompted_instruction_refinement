Cover Letter

Manuscript title: Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement: Bridging Manual Prompting Techniques
and Automatic Prompt Optimization
Manuscript type: Negative Result
Corresponding author: Quang-Vinh Dang (British University Vietnam), vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn

Dear Editor,

We submit our manuscript, "Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement: Bridging Manual Prompting
Techniques and Automatic Prompt Optimization," for consideration by IEEE Access under the
"Negative Result" manuscript type.

The paper reports a pre-registered go/no-go pilot testing Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement
(MPIR), a rubric-guided layer intended to refine automatic-prompt-optimization (APO) output for
large language models. An initial evaluation using a closed, more capable model (GPT-4o) to judge
and refine prompts for a weaker target model (GPT-3.5-turbo) reported per-task gains on a majority
of Big-Bench Hard tasks, but a pooled effect that did not reach statistical significance -- and a
subsequent audit found its validation stage had scored candidates against non-held-out data. To
determine whether the apparent gains reflected the refinement mechanism itself or the closed
model's capability advantage over its target, we reran the pilot with a single small open-weight
model (Qwen3-1.7B) performing every role -- target, optimizer, and refinement judge -- on the three
tasks where the original evaluation's effect was largest. The effect did not reproduce: across all
nine tested (optimizer, task) configurations, the refinement stage's output was byte-identical to
the unrefined baseline, and formal statistical tests (McNemar, GEE) returned no measurable effect
in any of them.

We believe this fits IEEE Access and its "Negative Result" category specifically, for three
reasons. First, the finding is a rigorously established boundary condition, not merely an
unremarkable absence of effect: we trace the null result to a general defect in the refinement
algorithm itself (it never scored the pre-refinement prompt as a baseline, so it could not have
reported a null result even where one existed) and to two concrete failure modes in how a small
model handles meta-prompting, each confirmed against live model output rather than assumed.
Second, the result is directly actionable for practitioners: it identifies a specific, common
deployment pattern (a single small open-weight model serving multiple roles) under which a
popular class of cheap refinement layers should not be expected to help, and it identifies a
narrower, lower-cost alternative (supplying a single worked exemplar to an otherwise exemplar-free
optimizer) that our own pilot data suggests may close more of the accuracy gap than the
refinement layer under test. Third, the paper documents seven real correctness defects found only
by running the pipeline against a live model rather than a mocked one, each of general relevance to
anyone building similar meta-prompting or refinement pipelines.

For full transparency: an earlier version of this work, evaluated under a different (closed-model)
configuration later found to have a validation-data leak, was submitted to and desk-rejected by a
different journal (AI Open) prior to this rebuild. The manuscript submitted here is a substantially
revised and independently re-evaluated version reporting a different, corrected finding, not a
resubmission of that earlier draft.

All code, configuration files, and per-example predictions underlying every number reported in
this paper are publicly available at
https://github.com/millennials92/meta_prompted_instruction_refinement, consistent with IEEE
Access's emphasis on replicability.

We confirm that this manuscript is not currently under review at any other venue, and that all
authors have approved its submission to IEEE Access.

Thank you for your consideration.
