ABSTRACT
Meta-Prompted Instruction Renement (MPIR) is a post-hoc layer that asks a
language model to score an automatically optimized prompt against a seven-criterion rubric, rewrite
it, and keep the rewrite only if it beats the original on held-out questions. An earlier evaluation
with a closed rener reported mixed gains, but audits found a validation leak and an unscored
baseline, and a small pilot left its renement rounds, statistics and answer-extraction harness open
to criticism. We report a re-test whose decision rules were xed in a time-stamped protocol. We
repaired the renement loop, logged every round, and rened 45 base prompts (PromptWizard,
APE and ProTeGi on nine Big-Bench Hard tasks, up to three seeds) with Qwen3-1.7B as target
and rener. The layer changed the prompt in 1 of 80 runs and won in 0 of 45 cells, mainly because
the small rener rarely produced a usable candidate that kept the answer format. Identical prompts
disagree on 6.5% of examples between scorings, and one answer-format exemplar raised the strict
accuracy of exemplar-free prompts by +19 and +42 points. In an exploratory probe, a 235B-
parameter rener won in 4 of 9 cells under strict scoring, but most of these gains reverse under
lenient scoring and a rubric-free rewrite reaches similar gains, so they do not isolate the rubric.
Conclusions are limited to Qwen3 targets, nine tasks and the optimizers tested.

1. INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) are used for translation
[1], summarization [2] and question answering [3] (see
[4] for a survey), and are being applied in domains such
as law and medicine [5], [6]. Their accuracy depends on
the prompt, and small changes in phrasing or formatting
can change it substantially [7], [8]. Writing and tuning
prompts is therefore a practical bottleneck for reliable
deployment.
Two families of approaches address that bottleneck.
Manual prompting applies heuristics distilled from prac-
tice, such as chain-of-thought reasoning [9], role assign-
ment [10] and few-shot demonstration [11]; these are
interpretable but need expertise and trial and error [12],
[13]. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) searches
the space of prompts with the help of an LLM, using
critique, mutation, evolution or optimizer-style proposal
loops [14][18]; it scales, but the prompts it produces
are often less structured and harder to interpret than
human-written ones [19]. A natural idea, which we
call Meta-Prompted Instruction Renement (MPIR),
is to add a cheap post-hoc stage after any APO run:
ask an LLM to score the optimized prompt against a
rubric of manual heuristics, ask it to rewrite the prompt
accordingly, and keep the rewrite only if it scores better
on held-out validation questions.
Whether such a layer helps matters in practice. It
costs a few model calls and can be added to any APO
pipeline, but if it does not help those calls are wasted,
and an ungated version can make prompts worse. A
clean test is not trivial: the rewrite is produced by a
model that may be no more capable than the target, the

2. RELATED WORK
2.1 MANUAL PROMPTING HEURISTICS
Prompting steers an LLM through in-context learning
without changing its weights [11], [21], and has been
described as a form of natural-language programming
[13]. Non-expert authors struggle because they get no
structural feedback and over-t to the few examples
they try [12]. Experienced authors rely on heuristics.
Few-shot demonstrations help [11], [22], though Min et
al. show that format, label space and input distribu-
tion matter more than label correctness [23]. Chain-of-
thought prompting decomposes multistep problems [9],
[24], and coherent, relevant reasoning steps matter more
than length [25], [26]. Role prompting assigns a persona
to guide reasoning [10], but its benet is inconsistent:
poorly aligned personas can hurt [27], and a large
study found that adding personas to system prompts
does not reliably improve performance [28]. Step-back
prompting abstracts key principles before solving [29]; a
behavioural benchmark of 35 LLMs found that it fosters
model-based behaviour in sequential decision tasks [30],
although this is weaker evidence for knowledge-intensive
reasoning than the original step-back results. Models
attend unevenly to information in long contexts [31],
[32], and vendor guidance reports recency eects for
some models while reporting no dierence for GPT-4-
class models [33]; whether either matters for the short
prompts studied here is an empirical question rather
than a premise. Self-consistency, a decoding-time heuris-
tic, samples several reasoning paths and votes [34];
2.2 AUTOMATIC PROMPT OPTIMIZATION AND
META-PROMPTING
APO methods generate, evaluate and select prompts
iteratively [19]. APE induces instructions from input-
output demonstrations [14]; ProTeGi treats LLM cri-
tique as a textual gradient and runs a beam search [15];
EvoPrompt applies evolutionary operators [16]; OPRO
conditions an LLM on a trajectory of scored prompts
[17]; PromptWizard combines critique-based renement,
example selection and self-generated reasoning [18].
DSPy, TextGrad, Self-Rene and Reexion generalize
the idea of optimizing or rening text with LLM feed-
back [35][38]. Meta-prompting supplies an LLM with
a template that structures the critique (see [39] for
a survey): PE2 uses a step-by-step diagnosis template
[40], dual-phase methods add a meta-instruction-guided
initialization [41], and PROPEL injects expert-derived
principles as priors [42]. Closest in spirit to MPIR,
a two-stage approach for few-shot relation extraction
rst searches and then locally edits the winner [43].
More recent work explores evolutionary reection [44],
error taxonomies [45], format optimization [46], debate-

2.3 LIMITS OF SELF-REFINEMENT AND OF LLM JUDGES
MPIR asks a model to critique and rewrite its own
prompt, which places it in the self-renement family [37],
[38]. Evidence on intrinsic self-correction is sobering:
LLMs cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without
external feedback [56], and small models need strong
veriers to benet from correction [57]. MPIR's critic
is also an LLM judge, and LLM judges show position,
verbosity and self-preference biases [58][60]. Finally,
benchmark accuracy depends on prompt format and on
how answers are extracted [8], [61], [62], which matters
here because MPIR's gate and our metrics both rely on a
delimiter-based extraction. These strands predict that a
1.7B model rening its own prompts may fail for reasons
unrelated to the rubric, which is why our design varies
the rener, validates the judge and re-scores answers
leniently.
2.4 REPORTING NEGATIVE RESULTS
Reproducibility programmes [63] and guidance on re-
porting experimental results [64] call for budgets, vari-
ance and full protocols. Variance across seeds and data
splits can exceed the eect of a method [65], signicance
testing in NLP needs care [66], and small test sets leave
studies under-powered [67]. We follow this guidance by
xing decision rules in advance, reporting per-cell paired
dierences with intervals rather than tests that cannot
be informative, and quantifying the noise oor and the
minimum detectable eect.

3. THE MPIR LAYER UNDER TEST
MPIR is a three-stage cycle applied to a prompt P0
that an upstream APO method has already produced:
(1) a language model scores P0 against a seven-criteria
rubric of manual prompting heuristics and recommends
improvements; (2) it rewrites P0 accordingly; (3) the
rewrite is scored on validation questions and kept only
if it beats the incumbent (Figure 1). We describe it here
as the object under test, not as a contribution.

the best validated prompt is returned. In the legacy
loop every round starts from P0; the repaired variants
of Section 3.2 sample the rener and, in the chained

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION
Let (Qv, Av) = {(qi, ai)}, i = 1..M, be a validation set
of M question-answer pairs and A(L, P) the accuracy of
the target model L on it under prompt P. Let R(P0) be
the set of candidate prompts that the renement loop
produces from P0. MPIR returns
Pnal = argmax over P' in {P0} ∪R(P0) of A(L, P'),
with ties resolved in favour of P0 (a candidate must
be strictly better). Including P0 in the candidate set is
what makes it possible for the procedure to conclude
that no renement helps.

3.3 THE SEVEN-CRITERIA RUBRIC
The rubric was assembled from the prompting literature
[68], [69], industry guidance [33], [70][72] and iterative
experimentation on BBH-style tasks. It scores a prompt
from 1 to 5 on: (1) Role Prompting, (2) Step-Back Rea-
soning, (3) Guided Chain of Thought, (4) Instruction
and Separation, (5) Output Format with Examples, (6)
Worked Reasoning in Examples and (7) Conclusion, for
a total out of 35. The evidence behind the criteria is
uneven. Chain-of-thought and demonstrations are well
supported [9], [23], [26]. Role prompting is the least
supported, since its benet is inconsistent [27], [28]. The
Conclusion criterion extrapolates positional-bias results
from long contexts [31], [32] to short prompts, and
the vendor page we cite for it states that instruction
placement made no dierence for GPT-4-class models
[33]. Industry sources are practitioner guidance rather
than empirical evidence; they are living documents, so

3.4 EVALUATION, REFINEMENT AND OUTPUT GUARDS
The evaluation meta-prompt (Figure 2) asks the rening
model to apply the rubric to the prompt and report
scores, strengths, weaknesses and 7-10 suggestions. The
renement request (Figure 3) asks for a rewrite wrapped
in <START> and <END>; in chained mode it also lists
the earlier candidates with their validation accuracy and
asks the model not to repeat them. Three output guards
exist, and each outcome is logged per round. A response
without delimiters is a fallback: it cannot be validated,
and the pilot's code returned P0 in that case, which
ties the baseline by construction. A candidate contain-
ing phrases verbatim from the evaluation template, or
APE's induction template, is an echo and is rejected
before validation. A candidate textually identical to
the incumbent, to P0 or to an earlier candidate is a
duplicate. Only the remaining candidates are validated.
The renement request of V1 and V2 does not ask the
rener to keep the answer-format line (the free-rewrite
prompt does), and a candidate is scored exactly as
written; this asymmetry matters for small reners and
is examined in Section 5.2.
3.5 THE VALIDATION GATE
A candidate is accepted only if its accuracy on the 25
validation questions strictly exceeds the incumbent's.
With 25 questions accuracy moves in steps of 4 per-
centage points, and one question of serving noise equals
a step, so acceptance is rare by design. Whether the
gate or the candidates explain the absence of accepted
renements is research question RQ2, which we answer
by scoring every candidate on the full test split (the
oracle analysis) and by re-applying other acceptance
rules oine (Section 5.4).

The target model is Qwen3-1.7B [73] with thinking

The rening model is the same model in the main grid.

4.5 METRICS
Accuracy is the share of test examples whose delimited
answer equals the gold answer after removing paren-
theses, whitespace and case (strict extraction, as in
the pilot). Because strict extraction penalizes correct
reasoning that is wrapped badly, we add a lenient
accuracy computed oine from the saved raw outputs:
the response counts as correct if the delimited span (or,
if there is none, the last 300 characters) names exactly
one option and it is the gold one. Both are reported;
strict extraction remains primary because MPIR's gate
uses it.
4.6 STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
For every cell we report the paired dierence in test
accuracy between the nal MPIR prompt and P0 with
a 95% paired-bootstrap interval over test examples.
Cells with identical prompts have a dierence of zero
by construction, apart from serving noise. Across cells
we report counts of wins, the mean dierence and a
hierarchical bootstrap interval that resamples tasks and
then the cells within each selected task. The noise
oor is the distribution of accuracy dierences between
repeated scorings of the same prompt, and the minimum
detectable eect at 80% power and α = 0.05 follows from
the paired discordance rate [66], [67]. Intervals use the
percentile bootstrap [76] and, for single accuracies, the
Wilson interval [77].
4.7 HARNESS DEFECTS AND DEVIATIONS
Running against a live model exposed defects that
mocked tests could not (truncation of long responses,
answer normalization, delimiter mismatches in expert
exemplars, template mismatches between an optimizer's
own and MPIR's evaluation harness, two echo patterns
and the unscored baseline). All are described with their
magnitude in Appendix C and were xed before the
numbers reported here were produced.

candidate that beats P0 by 3 points or more (0 and

format line (Section 5.2), so the gure largely reects
format loss. Candidates that keep the format are still not
better on average: -24.9 points for the format-protected
1.7B candidates of V1P and V2P (20 candidates, none
beating P0 by 3 points or more), -8.8 points for the 1.7B
rewrite (33 candidates) and -4.0 points for the Qwen3-
235B candidates (58; best candidate per cell +4.0 points
on average, 4 cells at +3 or more). D3 requires the
best candidate to beat P0 by 3 points in at least 3
of 9 cells while the gated nal prompt does not; it is
not met for the 1.7B rener. Validation accuracy tracks
test accuracy across candidates (Spearman 0.92 over
275 oracle-scored candidates of all reners), but this is
driven by candidates that score zero; among 1.7B-rener

question gate is noisy (Section 5.7). Table 8 re-applies
other acceptance rules oine to the same candidates;

strong reners dier, one belongs to the target's family

family and reasoning budget are confounded, and the
experiment is an upper-bound probe, not a controlled

5.7 SERVING NOISE AND MINIMUM DETECTABLE EFFECT
Each of the nine pilot base prompts was scored ve
times on the test and validation splits (identical prompt,
greedy decoding). Test accuracy varies with a standard
deviation of 1.4 points and a range of 3.3 points per
prompt on average; between two scorings of the same
prompt 6.5% of examples change outcome on average
(up to 16.6%), and the pilot and present scorings of the
same prompt dier by up to 7.0 points. This is larger
than the 2% assumed in the rst submission and is the
reason a 25-question validation split is noisy. For 200 test
examples the minimum detectable paired eect at 80%
power and α = 0.05 is 5.0 points if only serving noise
separates two prompts (paired discordance 6.5%), and
6.3 to 8.9 points if two dierent prompts disagree on 10-
20% of examples, as candidates here do. The strict gains
of the strong rener (+30, +29, +12, +11 points) are
above this oor; eects of a few points are not detectable
at these test sizes.
5.8 DOES THE RUBRIC JUDGE MEASURE ANYTHING?
(RQ4)
The 1.7B judge gives a parseable total for 93% of
prompts and scores them 7.2 points higher than the
reference judge on average; its totals for the same
unchanged P0 vary with a standard deviation of 4.7
points across sampled rounds. Both judges' totals cor-
relate only weakly with accuracy (median within-cell
Spearman 0.27 and 0.27 with test accuracy), and the two
judges agree with each other at 0.38. The rubric score
is therefore at best a weak signal of prompt quality. No
human ratings were collected; the reference judge is a
stronger model, not a person, so this analysis bounds
the judge's usefulness but does not validate the rubric
against human judgement.
5.9 EFFECT OF THE ORIGINAL DEFECT
The original loop accepted the rst round's outcome
whatever it scored. With the 1.7B rener and nine cells
(MPIR seed 1, independent variant, oracle-scored can-
didates), applying that rule gives a mean test-accuracy
change of -13.6 points relative to P0 when a fallback
or echo round returns P0, as the pilot's code did:
the prompt changed in 4 of 9 cells and was worse
than P0 in 4, better in 0. Under the literal reading of
protocol G6 (the rst validated candidate is accepted
unconditionally) the mean change is -29.2 points. Two
cells account for most of either loss, the repaired loop
changes accuracy by -0.9 points on the same candidates,
and the gure applies to this rener only; its eect with
the original GPT-4o rener is unknown, because that
conguration was not rerun.

6.1 WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS

6.2 PRACTICAL GUIDANCE, SCOPED TO WHAT WAS
TESTED
The following points follow from these experiments,
with Qwen3 models, nine BBH tasks and the optimizers
listed; they are not general rules. (i) Before adding a
renement layer, inspect how answers are extracted and
check a lenient metric: one worked example of the answer
format moved strict accuracy of exemplar-free prompts
by +19 and +42 points (APE, ProTeGi), and +2 and
+10 points under lenient extraction. (ii) Do not accept a
model's rewrite of a prompt without scoring the original
prompt as a baseline; with a 1.7B rener the uncorrected
loop lowered accuracy by about 14 points on average
in our nine cells (one MPIR seed). (iii) Gate on held-
out data, but note that identical prompts scored twice
dier on 6.5% of examples, so dierences of a few points
on a few hundred questions are noise. (iv) When the
rening model is as small as the target, expect most
rewrites to be unusable (echoes, missing delimiters, a
dropped answer format) and monitor that rate; protect
the answer-format line explicitly.
6.3 THREATS TO VALIDITY
6.3.1 Internal Validity
The serving stack is not deterministic: repeated scoring
of one prompt spans 3.3 points on average (standard
deviation 1.4), which the gate and the minimum de-
tectable eect (5.0 points) take into account but cannot
remove. The inference server was restarted several times
and its memory settings were changed during the study;
cached scores and generations were kept across restarts,
so a few interrupted runs combine scorings taken under
dierent settings, with dierences of the order of the
measured noise. The main grid has 80 runs instead of the
planned 135, oracle scoring covers MPIR seed 1 only, the
strong-rener runs use one MPIR seed (a second for one
rener), and the format-protected variants cover only
part of the nine Tier-1 seed-42 cells (Section 5.2). Two
audit ndings, the format asymmetry of V1 and V2 and
a lenient parser that rejected bare letters, were found
late and corrected after the main grid had run; the main
grid was not re-run with protection. The follow-up runs
and the protected variants were added after results were
seen. The APE prompt for hyperbaton is a degenerate
artifact of our implementation (Appendix A), and many
APE and ProTeGi base prompts are weak, which limits
what rening them can show.
6.3.2 External Validity
One target model family and size was used in every run.
Tier 1 was chosen from a biased statistic and its tasks
were also used while the rubric was developed; Tier 2
is a random draw of six tasks from 24, with one split
seed. The strong-rener experiment covers nine cells.
The original closed-model conguration, including GPT-
4o as rener, was not rerun; GPT-6 Luna is a dierent
model and not a replication of it.

6.3.3 Construct and Conclusion Validity
Strict answer extraction partly measures output for-
matting, and the lenient metric is an approximation.
The rubric and its judge are weakly related to accuracy
(Section 5.8), no human ratings were collected, and the
rubric's criteria were not ablated. The statistics are
descriptive by design: with identical prompts in most
cells, tests of dierences would be uninformative, the
nine pilot cells share three tasks and three optimizers
and are not independent, and the cell-level wins of the
strong reners carry no multiplicity correction.
7. CONCLUSION
We re-tested a rubric-guided renement layer for au-
tomatically optimized prompts after repairing its re-
nement loop and xing the decision rules in advance.
With a 1.7B model rening its own prompts the layer
produced no improvement in 45 cells (one of 80 runs
accepted a candidate), mainly because that rener rarely
produces a usable, format-preserving candidate. A much
larger rener obtained strict-metric gains in several cells,
but most reverse under lenient scoring and a rubric-free
rewrite reaches gains of the same kind, so the rubric
is not shown to be what helps; one gain, on a single
task, holds under both metrics. With a 1.7B rener the
uncorrected loop would have reduced accuracy by about
14 points (its eect with the original GPT-4o rener is
unknown), strict answer extraction accounts for much
of the apparent weakness of exemplar-free baselines,
and identical prompts do not score identically on this
serving stack. These conclusions are limited to Qwen3-
based targets, nine tasks and the optimizers tested, and
the rst evaluation's GPT-4o results remain unveried.
Code, per-round logs and generations are public.
