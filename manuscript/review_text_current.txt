ABSTRACT
Meta-Prompted Instruction Renement (MPIR) is a post-hoc layer that asks a
language model to score an automatically optimized prompt against a seven-criteria rubric, rewrite
it, and keep the rewrite only if it beats the original on held-out questions. A rst evaluation with
a closed rener reported mixed gains, but later audits found a validation leak and an unscored
baseline, and a pilot with a single small model showed no eect while leaving its renement rounds,
statistics and harness open to criticism. We report a pre-specied re-test. We repaired the renement
loop so that rounds are distinct, logged every round, and ran 45 base prompts (PromptWizard,
APE and ProTeGi on nine Big-Bench Hard tasks, up to three seeds) with Qwen3-1.7B as target
and rener. The layer changed the prompt in 1 of 80 runs and won in 0 of 45 cells. Candidates
were on average worse than the original prompt, the validation gate tracked test accuracy, and
the uncorrected loop would have cost about 14 accuracy points. Identical prompts disagree on 6%
of examples between scorings, and a single answer-format exemplar outperformed any renement.
With a 235B-parameter rener the layer won in 4 of 9 cells, but the gains coincide with a repaired
answer format, so they do not isolate the rubric. Conclusions are limited to Qwen3 targets, nine
tasks and the optimizers tested.

1. INTRODUCTION
The prompt is the specication, the interface and the
test xture of an LLM-based component at once, and
unlike source code it cannot be checked by a compiler.
Large language models (LLMs) perform strongly on
tasks such as translation [1], summarization [2] and
question answering [3] (see [4] for a survey), yet small
changes in phrasing or formatting can change their ac-
curacy substantially [5], [6]. Prompt design has therefore
become a bottleneck for reliable deployment in domains
such as law and medicine [7], [8].
Two families of approaches address that bottleneck.
Manual prompting applies heuristics distilled from prac-
tice, such as chain-of-thought reasoning [9], role assign-
ment [10] and few-shot demonstration [11]; these are
interpretable but need expertise and trial and error [12],
[13]. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) searches
the space of prompts with the help of an LLM, using
critique, mutation, evolution or optimizer-style proposal
loops [14][18]; it scales but may forfeit the structure
that human-written prompts carry [19]. A natural idea,
which we call Meta-Prompted Instruction Renement
(MPIR), is to add a cheap post-hoc stage after any APO
run: ask an LLM to score the optimized prompt against a
rubric of manual heuristics, ask it to rewrite the prompt
accordingly, and keep the rewrite only if it scores better
on held-out validation questions.
This paper does not propose MPIR as a new method.

2. RELATED WORK
A. MANUAL PROMPTING HEURISTICS
Prompting steers an LLM through in-context learning
without changing its weights [11], [21], and has been
described as a form of natural-language programming
[13]. Non-expert authors struggle because they get no
structural feedback and over-t to the few examples
they try [12]. Experienced authors rely on heuristics.
Few-shot demonstrations help [11], [22], though Min et
al. show that format, label space and input distribu-
tion matter more than label correctness [23]. Chain-of-
thought prompting decomposes multistep problems [9],
[24], and coherent, relevant reasoning steps matter more
than length [25], [26]. Role prompting assigns a persona
to guide reasoning [10], but its benet is inconsistent:
poorly aligned personas can hurt [27], and a large
study found that adding personas to system prompts
does not reliably improve performance [28]. Step-back
prompting abstracts key principles before solving [29];
a behavioural benchmark of 35 LLMs found that it fos-
ters model-based behaviour in sequential decision tasks
[30], which is weaker support for knowledge-intensive
reasoning than the original step-back evidence. Models
attend unevenly to information in long contexts [31],
[32], and vendor guidance reports recency eects [33];
whether either matters for the short prompts studied
here is an empirical question rather than a premise. Self-
consistency, a decoding-time heuristic, samples several
reasoning paths and votes [34]; MPIR does not use it.

C. LIMITS OF SELF-REFINEMENT AND OF LLM JUDGES
MPIR asks a model to critique and rewrite its own
prompt, which places it in the self-renement family [36],
[37]. Evidence on intrinsic self-correction is sobering:
LLMs cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without
external feedback [55], and small models need strong
veriers to benet from correction [56]. MPIR's critic
is also an LLM judge, and LLM judges show position,
verbosity and self-preference biases [57][59]. Finally,
benchmark accuracy depends on prompt format and on
how answers are extracted [6], [60], [61], which matters
here because MPIR's gate and our metrics both rely on a
delimiter-based extraction. These strands predict that a
1.7B model rening its own prompts may fail for reasons
unrelated to the rubric, which is why our design varies
the rener, validates the judge and re-scores answers
leniently.
D. REPORTING NEGATIVE RESULTS
Reproducibility programmes [62] and guidance on re-
porting experimental results [63] call for budgets, vari-
ance and full protocols. Variance across seeds and data
splits can exceed the eect of a method [64], signicance
testing in NLP needs care [65], and small test sets leave
studies under-powered [66]. We follow this guidance by
xing decision rules in advance, reporting per-cell paired
dierences with intervals rather than tests that cannot
be informative, and quantifying the noise oor and the
minimum detectable eect.

3. THE MPIR LAYER UNDER TEST
MPIR is a three-stage cycle applied to a prompt P0
that an upstream APO method has already produced:
(1) a language model scores P0 against a seven-criteria
rubric of manual prompting heuristics and recommends
improvements; (2) it rewrites P0 accordingly; (3) the
rewrite is scored on validation questions and kept only
if it beats the incumbent (Figure 1). We describe it here
as the object under test, not as a contribution.

C. THE SEVEN-CRITERIA RUBRIC
The rubric was assembled from the prompting literature
[67], [68], industry guidance [33], [69][71] and iterative
experimentation on BBH-style tasks. It scores a prompt
from 1 to 5 on: (1) Role Prompting, (2) Step-Back Rea-
soning, (3) Guided Chain of Thought, (4) Instruction
and Separation, (5) Output Format with Examples, (6)
Worked Reasoning in Examples and (7) Conclusion, for
a total out of 35. The evidence behind the criteria is
uneven. Chain-of-thought and demonstrations are well
supported [9], [23], [26]. Role prompting is the least
supported, since its benet is inconsistent [27], [28]. The
Conclusion criterion extrapolates positional-bias results
from long contexts [31], [32] to short prompts, and
the vendor page we cite for it states that instruction
placement made no dierence for GPT-4-class models
[33]. Industry sources are practitioner guidance rather
than empirical evidence; they are living documents, so
the reference list gives archived copies and access dates.
The rubric text used in the pilot was internally incon-
sistent: it announced 've dened criteria', asked for 'all
6 criteria' and 'Total Score: X/25' while listing seven
criteria. We found this during the audit. The primary
variants use a corrected text (seven criteria, X/35);
variant V2L keeps the original text as a sensitivity check.

E. THE VALIDATION GATE
A candidate is accepted only if its accuracy on the 25
validation questions strictly exceeds the incumbent's.
With 25 questions accuracy moves in steps of 4 per-
centage points, and one question of serving noise equals
a step, so acceptance is rare by design. Whether the
gate or the candidates explain the absence of accepted
renements is research question RQ2, which we answer
by scoring every candidate on the full test split (the
oracle analysis) and by re-applying other acceptance
rules oine (Section 5.4).

C. MODELS AND SERVING
The target model is Qwen3-1.7B [72] with thinking
disabled and greedy decoding, at most 1024 new tokens.
The rening model is the same model in the main
grid and Qwen3-4B-AWQ or Qwen3-8B-AWQ in the
capability experiment; meta-prompt calls allow 1500
(evaluation) and 3000 (renement) new tokens. Rener
sampling uses temperature 0.7. Everything is served
with vLLM 0.28.0 under WSL2 on one 16 GB RTX 5000
Ada GPU, bf16 weights for the 1.7B model, a maximum
context of 8192 tokens (Appendix C). Greedy decoding
is not bit-reproducible under continuous batching, so we
measure the resulting noise (Section 5.7).
D. BASE OPTIMIZERS AND CONDITIONS
APE and ProTeGi are clean-room implementations of
the published algorithms [14], [15], run with few_shot_
count = 0, hence exemplar-free; ProTeGi scores every
successor on a full minibatch instead of using the original
bandit scheme. PromptWizard [18] is run with synthetic-
example generation disabled; its three in-context ex-

model-generated reasoning, and its generated expert
identity is not used at evaluation. Hyperparameters

uses the three BBH-author exemplars [20]; the rst
manuscript's statement that APE and ProTeGi also
used them was wrong. Reference conditions are zero-
shot CoT [24] and expert few-shot CoT. The free-rewrite
control (Appendix A.3) rewrites P0 with no rubric; V3
adds the gate.

6. DISCUSSION
A. WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS
With Qwen3-1.7B as target and rener, the rubric-
guided layer did not improve the prompts of any of
three optimizers on any of nine tasks: the nal prompt
equalled P0 in 79 of 80 runs, and by the pre-specied rule
the outcome is null (Section 5.3). That conclusion sur-
vives the points raised against the pilot. The renement
rounds were made genuinely distinct by sampling and by
history. The failure is not an artifact of the coarse gate,
because ungated candidates are on average clearly worse
than P0 and validation accuracy tracks test accuracy
(Section 5.4). It is not caused by the inconsistent rubric
text (Section 5.2), and a rubric-free gated rewrite does
no better (Section 5.5). Most renement attempts by
this rener never reach validation, which is a failure of
instruction following, not a verdict on the rubric.
The evidence for capability dependence is mixed and
should be read as a probe, not a measurement. A
much larger open-weight rener (Qwen3-235B-A22B)
produced signicant gains in several cells, while a strong
closed rener run with minimal reasoning (GPT-6 Luna)
did so in one (Section 5.6). The gains arise where the
base prompt fails for format reasons, and the accepted
candidates add an answer-format instruction and a
worked output example, the same lever that a single
exemplar delivers without any rener (Section 5.1). We
therefore cannot claim that the seven-criteria rubric
itself, as opposed to rewriting by a stronger model that
happens to repair the output format, is responsible.
Criterion-level ablations were not run, and the original
GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-turbo conguration was not re-
peated, so the rst evaluation's per-task gains remain
unexplained and should not be cited as evidence for
MPIR.
B. PRACTICAL GUIDANCE, SCOPED TO WHAT WAS TESTED
The following points follow from these experiments,
with Qwen3 models, nine BBH tasks and the optimizers
listed; they are not general rules. (i) Before adding a
renement layer, inspect how answers are extracted:
a single worked example of the answer format moved
strict accuracy of exemplar-free prompts by tens of
points here, far more than any renement. (ii) Do not
accept a model's rewrite of a prompt without scoring
the original prompt as a baseline; the uncorrected loop
lowered accuracy by about 14 points on average. (iii)
Gate on held-out data, but note that identical prompts
scored twice dier on 6% of examples, so dierences of
a few points on a few hundred questions are noise. (iv)
When the rening model is as small as the target, expect
most rewrites to be unusable (echoes, missing delimiters)
and monitor that rate; a larger rener reduces it.

1) Internal Validity
The serving stack is not deterministic: repeated scor-
ing of one prompt moves accuracy by 3.3 points on

The inference server was restarted several times and
its memory settings were changed during the study;
cached scores and generations were kept across restarts,
so a few interrupted runs combine scorings taken under
dierent settings, with dierences of the order of the
measured noise. Oracle scoring of all candidates covers

The APE prompt for hyperbaton is a degenerate artifact
of our implementation (Appendix A), which limits what
rening it can show.
2) External Validity
One target model family and size was used in every run.
Tier 1 was chosen from a biased statistic and Tier 2 is
a random draw of six tasks from 24, so the nine tasks
may not represent the other BBH tasks or other task
families. The strong-rener experiment covers nine cells.
The original closed-model conguration, including GPT-
4o as rener, was not rerun; GPT-6 Luna is a dierent
model and is not a replication of it.
3) Construct and Conclusion Validity
Strict answer extraction partly measures output format-
ting; we report lenient accuracy as a secondary metric.
The rubric and its judge are weakly related to accuracy
(Section 5.8), no human ratings were collected, and the
rubric's criteria were not ablated, so the study says
little about which criteria matter. The statistics are
descriptive by design: with identical prompts in most
cells, tests of dierences would be uninformative, and the
nine pilot cells share three tasks and three optimizers,
so they are not independent.
7. CONCLUSION
We re-tested a rubric-guided renement layer for auto-
matically optimized prompts after repairing its rene-
ment loop, adding gates, controls and diagnostics, and
xing the decision rules in advance. With a 1.7B model
rening its own prompts the layer produced no improve-
ment in 45 cells (one of 80 runs accepted a candidate).
The uncorrected loop of the rst evaluation would have
reduced accuracy, the strict answer extraction accounts
for much of the apparent weakness of exemplar-free
baselines, and identical prompts do not score identically
on this serving stack. A far larger rener can make the
layer improve some prompts, but the gains coincide with
a repaired answer format that a single exemplar also
provides, so they do not show that the rubric is what
helps. These conclusions are limited to Qwen3-based

APPENDIX. THE ORIGINAL EVALUATION
(EXPLORATORY; VALIDATION LEAK)
The rst evaluation used GPT-3.5-turbo as target and
GPT-4o as rener on all BBH tasks. It is reproduced
here only because the manuscript's framing referred to
it, and Reviewer comments asked for its numbers. Its
validation stage scored candidates on the optimizer's
own training examples and its loop never scored the
unrened prompt (Section 3.2), single runs at
temperature 0. The numbers are exploratory, are not
evidence for MPIR, and were not used to compute any
result in Section 5. The Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite check
used the same model as target and rener, with the
same defects; its baseline already averaged 92%, leaving
little headroom, and it was not analysed further.
The three pilot tasks ranked 1, 3 and 6 of 23 by the
original PW+MPIR gain (hyperbaton +21.8,
penguins_in_a_table +7.6, ruin_names +5.8 points).
Selecting tasks on the maximum of a noisy, leaked
statistic invites winner's-curse eects, which is why
Tier 2 of the revised design draws tasks at random.
