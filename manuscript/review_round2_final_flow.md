# Final flow review, round 2 (read start to finish, plaintext version)

Overall judgement. The science is sound and the hedging is generally good (scope in title, AI-labeller disclosures, exploratory labels, "we do not claim equivalence"). The weaknesses are narrative and redundancy. (a) The reader meets V0, V1, V2, V2L, V3, V1P, V2P, V2F and three refiners before any map of them exists; Table R.4, the only map, sits in 5.10. (b) The "why the first test could not answer the question" step is visible only in 5.2 and 5.3.1; the Introduction and abstract state the null and the cause in one clause. (c) The same figures recur in 4 to 6 places: 93-95% format loss, 60% (or 66%) rounds lost, 24 of 26, 6.5% flips, +19/+42, 4 of 9 cells, 5.0 MDE. (d) Review-history residue remains in the body, and the Introduction contradicts Section 4.1 about it. (e) Grammar artefacts from templated numbers: "1 cell(s)", "1 cells", "1 wins", "about 1 points".

Items are ordered by priority. Replacement text keeps every number as it appears in the manuscript, except where noted.

## 1. Abstract
Location: Abstract | "With the 1.7B model refining its own prompts"
Problem: states the null and its cause in one breath, does not say that the first test was uninformative; "1 cell(s)"; the +19/+42 exemplar result reads as part of the main chain; the one-run-per-condition caveat for the 235B probe is missing.
Replacement (whole abstract):
Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement (MPIR) asks a language model to score an optimized prompt against a seven-criterion rubric, rewrite it, and keep the rewrite only if it beats the original on validation questions. We re-test it after an earlier evaluation was found to have a validation leak, using a repaired, fully logged loop and decision rules fixed in advance with dated amendments. The scope is narrow: Qwen3-1.7B as target, 45 base prompts from three small-model optimizers on nine Big-Bench Hard tasks, many of them weak. With the 1.7B model refining its own prompts, the original treatment changed the prompt in 1 of 80 runs (exact 95% upper bound 5.8%), but 93-95% of its candidates had lost the answer format, so this test could not tell a failed idea from a failed treatment. A format- and example-preserving re-run (V2F) followed a positive control in which the gate accepted a known-good candidate in 24 of 26 cells. Candidates were then accepted in 15 of 44 runs, but the final prompts won in 1 cell and lost in 3 under a lenient metric validated on 400 AI-labelled responses. In exploratory probes with one run per condition, a 235B refiner won in 4 of 9 cells under strict scoring, mostly format repairs for which a rubric-free rewrite obtains gains of the same kind (paired difference +2.7 points, interval [-3.5, +9.7]); an 8B refiner changed the prompt in 1 of 9 runs. Separately, one answer-format exemplar changed strict accuracy by +19 (APE) and +42 (ProTeGi) points on average, mostly through the tag convention, and identical prompts disagree on 6.5% of examples between scorings. Conclusions are limited to Qwen3 targets, nine tasks and the optimizers tested.

## 5. Section 5.2, second paragraph (60% versus 66%, causal claim)
Location: 5.2 | "For the 1.7B refiner, 60% of the chained rounds"
Problem: the 60% here, 66% in the audit paragraph and in 5.11 look inconsistent; a 12-sentence block mixes the unprotected result, V1P/V2P and V2L; "An audit found a large part of the reason" is unclear.
Replacement:
For the 1.7B refiner, 337 of the 560 chained rounds (60%) ended in a fallback or echo and never reached validation (66% once the 30 duplicates are counted). Of the 193 validated candidates, 126 scored below the baseline, 66 tied and 1 scored higher, so most validated candidates are far worse, not tied. The main reason is that the refinement request does not ask the refiner to keep the answer-format line: 93% of the oracle-scored V1 and 95% of the oracle-scored V2 candidates (MPIR seed 1) lack it and score exactly zero under strict extraction, whereas candidates of the rubric-free rewrite (which has the line appended in code) and of the protected variants always carry it. The unprotected rows of Table 6 therefore measure the refiner's failure to keep the format at least as much as any failure of the rubric. To separate the two we added V1P and V2P, which append the format line to every candidate. They still reached validation in only 30% (V1P, 70 rounds) and 27% (V2P, 56 rounds) of rounds, and 5 of their 36 validated candidates beat the baseline on validation. They were run on the nine Tier-1 seed-42 cells and finished on 5 (V1P) and 8 (V2P) of them; the rest were not completed for compute reasons. The original, inconsistent rubric text (V2L) was no better: 0 accepted candidates in 63 rounds and 63% of rounds lost to fallbacks or echoes, so the inconsistency in the rubric text is not what limits the 1.7B refiner.

What the candidates keep. Among the 120 V1 and 223 V2 candidates that were not echoes, none keeps the exact answer-format sentence (the 93-95% above counts oracle-scored candidates that lack the answer tag altogether), and only 8% and 7% contain a bare-tag worked example. ## 7. Section 5.2, audit paragraph
Location: 5.2 | "Audit of the round classification. The echo guard"
Problem: 300-word paragraph of diagnostics that interrupts the story; repeats the 60/66% figures; the 230 of 236 detail does not support any claim.
Replacement:
Audit of the round classification. The echo guard (five marker phrases) and the missing-delimiter rule decide 406 of the 749 V1 and V2 rounds. A language-model assistant, not a human rater, labelled a random sample of 40 echo and 40 fallback rounds (1.7B refiner, V1, V2, V1P, V2P) from the head and tail of each logged text, so this is a screening audit; the sample and labels are released for re-checking. 4 of 40 sampled echoes were false positives (rate 10%, one-sided 95% upper bound 21%). Of the sampled fallbacks, 25 never presented a refined prompt, 10 presented one without a closing tag, none was cut off by a length limit, and 6 of 40 (15%) were recoverable by a tolerant parser. Truncation does not explain the fallbacks: the median fallback response is 631 tokens long against the 3000-token limit (90th percentile 986), and only 2 of 1498 refinement responses come within 10% of it (the judge call is closer to its 1500-token limit, 77 of 1498, which can cut a critique but not the rewrite that follows). The guards are therefore mostly right (about 90% precision for echoes), the dominant failure is a refiner that critiques instead of rewriting, and a tolerant parser would recover only a small minority of the lost rounds.

## 9. Section 5.3.1, positive control and bare-tag control
Location: 5.3.1 | "Positive control. For each of the 45 base prompts"
Problem: "about 1 points"; the 24 of 26 denominator is chosen by an outcome on test, and the abstract, 6.1 and 7 quote it as if it were a detection rate; two sets of exemplar numbers (+19.4/+20.0 here, +19.5/+42.3 in 5.1) are explained only at the end.
Replacement (two paragraphs):
Positive control. For each of the 45 base prompts we offered the gate a candidate known to be good under strict scoring, P0 plus the first worked example of the BBH authors' prompt (the intervention of Section 5.1), and applied the usual rule, strict improvement of the 25-question validation score over P0. The gate accepted it in 29 of 45 cells overall: 15 of 15 ProTeGi cells, 9 of 15 APE cells and 5 of 15 PromptWizard cells; the PromptWizard prompts already contain bare-tag examples, so those acceptances do not test a known-good candidate. Of the 26 APE and ProTeGi cells in which the exemplar raises strict test accuracy, the gate accepted 24, and it never accepted the exemplar where the exemplar did not help on test (0 cases). Those 26 cells are defined by the test outcome, so 24 of 26 is a check on sensitivity to a large effect, not an acceptance rate for unknown candidates. The gate and harness can therefore detect a large strict-metric effect when one is offered, and the null of V2 is not an artefact of a gate that accepts nothing.

Bare-tag control. The exemplar adds a worked chain of thought as well as the tag. Replacing it by one sentence that shows only the final-answer tag raises strict test accuracy by +19.4 points for APE and +34.2 points for ProTeGi, against +20.0 and +42.8 points for the full exemplar (the paired interval excludes zero in 10 of 15 APE and 15 of 15 ProTeGi cells for the bare tag). Most of the effect is therefore the tag convention, and the worked reasoning adds about 1 point for APE and 9 for ProTeGi. The effect is heterogeneous: it ranges from losses of up to 45 points on two APE prompts to gains of more than 70 points on ProTeGi prompts. These values were rescored in the second-revision session; Section 5.1 reports +19.5 and +42.3 from the earlier session.

## 10. Section 5.3.1, "What this shows" (overclaim)
Location: 5.3.1 | "behaves like a noisy search whose accepted candidates cannot be told apart from chance"
Problem: no random-acceptance baseline was run, so "cannot be told apart from chance" is not tested; the limits are a long run-on sentence.
Replacement:
What this shows. Giving the 1.7B refiner a treatment that can succeed raises the acceptance rate far above that of V2 (15 of 44 runs against 1 of 80), so the V2 null was in part a property of the unprotected treatment. It does not, however, produce reliable gains: against its own baseline the accepted candidates win in 4 cells under strict scoring and in 1 under lenient scoring, with 3 significant lenient losses. One reading is that a 1.7B model refining its own prompt behaves like a noisy search on a 25-question gate; we did not test this against a random-acceptance baseline. Three limits apply. First, V2F is a hybrid treatment: it changes the gate's baseline and re-attaches the format and the example in code, and there is no rubric-free counterpart under the same protection (V3 protects the format line but not the baseline or the example), so the lenient losses cannot be attributed to the rubric. Second, V2F was run only with the 1.7B refiner, one seed and 44 cells, and its decision rule was scaled post hoc to that number of cells. Third, the positive control shows that the gate accepts a hand-built candidate, not that a refiner can produce one. Finally, ProTeGi returned the seed instruction plus the format line in 15 of its 15 cells (Appendix G), which is why its exemplar effect is the largest; for ProTeGi the experiments test the refinement of a seed prompt, not of an optimized one.

Strict accuracy is, however, a poor judge of these gains. Lenient extraction was applied to every run and both MPIR seeds are reported; nothing was selected, and the seed-2 runs have only their final prompt scored on test. ## 14. Section 5.11 (Cost), closing paragraph
Location: 5.11 | "What the layer costs, and whether the 'cheap layer' argument"

Replacement:
A run issues 14 refiner calls (7 for V3) and generates about 12 thousand refiner tokens, plus about 134 target calls for validation and 356 for the two test scorings that any evaluation needs anyway; in absolute terms this is small next to the base optimizers, which score many candidates on minibatches. ## 15. Section 6.1, three opening paragraphs
Location: 6.1 | "With Qwen3-1.7B as target and refiner, the rubric-guided layer"
Problem: restates Section 5 numbers almost verbatim; "so the treatment can now succeed and still does not help" is unhedged; the measurement paragraph overgeneralizes from one accepted candidate plus V2F lenient losses.
Replacement (three paragraphs):
With Qwen3-1.7B as target and refiner, the rubric-guided layer as originally specified (V2) did not improve the prompts of any of three optimizers on any of nine tasks: the final prompt equalled P0 in 79 of 80 runs (exact one-sided 95% upper bound on the acceptance rate 5.8%), and by the decision rule fixed in advance the outcome is null (Section 5.3). The treatment, not only the idea, was at fault: the refinement request did not protect the answer-format line, so 93-95% of the candidates lost it and scored zero under strict extraction, and about 60% of rounds never produced a usable candidate. We therefore re-ran the grid with a format- and example-preserving treatment (V2F, 44 cells) after a positive control showed that the gate and the harness accept a known-good candidate in 24 of 26 cells in which it helps. Acceptance rose to 15 of 44 runs, but against its own baseline the final prompts win in 4 cells under strict scoring and in 1 under lenient scoring, with 3 significant lenient losses. In this setup the treatment can succeed and still does not help. This is a result about a 1.7B model refining its own, mostly weak, prompts, and it says little about rubric-guided refinement by a capable model.

The evidence on more capable refiners is exploratory (nine cells, one or two runs per condition). Qwen3-235B produced significant strict gains in four cells in two MPIR seeds and GPT-6 Luna in one. Most of these gains repair the answer tag on base prompts that reason about as well as before; under lenient extraction, which we validated on 400 labelled responses, they reverse in the first MPIR seed and vanish in the second. A rubric-free gated rewrite by the same refiner reaches strict gains of the same kind, and the paired difference between the rubric-guided and the rubric-free variant is +2.7 points with an interval [-3.5, +9.7] that includes zero (Section 5.6.1), so the data cannot separate 'the rubric adds nothing' from 'the rubric adds a modest benefit'. The intermediate open-weight refiner (Section 5.6.2) does not change this picture. The one gain that survives both metrics, hyperbaton with PromptWizard, is a single cell on a base prompt that opens with a garbled instruction and scores near chance. Criterion-level ablations were not run and the original GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-turbo configuration was not repeated, so the first evaluation's per-task gains remain unexplained and should not be cited as evidence for MPIR.

A second lesson concerns measurement. Answer extraction decides what the gate rewards: in our runs a strict gate accepted rewrites that repaired the tag even where lenient accuracy did not improve, and exemplar-free baselines look far weaker under strict scoring than under lenient scoring. Reporting a lenient metric next to the strict one exposed both effects.
