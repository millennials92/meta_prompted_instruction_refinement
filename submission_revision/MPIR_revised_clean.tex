\begin{abstract}
Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement (MPIR) is a post-hoc layer that asks a language model to score an automatically optimized prompt against a seven-criterion rubric, rewrite it, and keep the rewrite only if it beats the original on held-out questions. An earlier evaluation with a closed refiner reported mixed gains, but audits found a validation leak and an unscored baseline, and a small pilot left its refinement rounds, statistics and answer-extraction harness open to criticism. We report a re-test whose decision rules were fixed in a time-stamped protocol. We repaired the refinement loop, logged every round, and refined 45 base prompts (PromptWizard, APE and ProTeGi on nine Big-Bench Hard tasks, up to three seeds) with Qwen3-1.7B as target and refiner. The layer changed the prompt in 1 of 80 runs and won in 0 of 45 cells, mainly because the small refiner rarely produced a usable candidate that kept the answer format. Identical prompts disagree on 6.5\% of examples between scorings, and one answer-format exemplar raised the strict accuracy of exemplar-free prompts by +19 and +42 points. In an exploratory probe, a 235B-parameter refiner won in 4 of 9 cells under strict scoring, but most of these gains reverse under lenient scoring and a rubric-free rewrite reaches similar gains, so they do not isolate the rubric. Conclusions are limited to Qwen3 targets, nine tasks and the optimizers tested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are used for translation \cite{vilar2023}, summarization \cite{goyal2023} and question answering \cite{kamalloo2023} (see \cite{qin2025} for a survey), and are being applied in domains such as law and medicine \cite{colombo2024,zhang2025}. Their accuracy depends on the prompt, and small changes in phrasing or formatting can change it substantially \cite{errica2025,sclar2024}. Writing and tuning prompts is therefore a practical bottleneck for reliable deployment.

Two families of approaches address that bottleneck. Manual prompting applies heuristics distilled from practice, such as chain-of-thought reasoning \cite{wei2022}, role assignment \cite{kong2024} and few-shot demonstration \cite{brown2020}; these are interpretable but need expertise and trial and error \cite{zamfirescupereira2023,reynolds2021}. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) searches the space of prompts with the help of an LLM, using critique, mutation, evolution or optimizer-style proposal loops \cite{zhou2023,pryzant2023,guo2024,yang2024,agarwal2025}; it scales, but the prompts it produces are often less structured and harder to interpret than human-written ones \cite{ramnath2025}. A natural idea, which we call Meta-Prompted Instruction Refinement (MPIR), is to add a cheap post-hoc stage after any APO run: ask an LLM to score the optimized prompt against a rubric of manual heuristics, ask it to rewrite the prompt accordingly, and keep the rewrite only if it scores better on held-out validation questions.

Whether such a layer helps matters in practice. It costs a few model calls and can be added to any APO pipeline, but if it does not help those calls are wasted, and an ungated version can make prompts worse. A clean test is not trivial: the rewrite is produced by a model that may be no more capable than the target, the gate uses a small validation set, and benchmark accuracy depends on how answers are extracted. We therefore report a pre-specified test, and we treat a null result as informative.

The idea was first evaluated with GPT-4o as the refining model and GPT-3.5-turbo as the target on the tasks of Big-Bench Hard (BBH) \cite{suzgun2023}. That evaluation reported per-task gains over PromptWizard \cite{agarwal2025} but a pooled gain that was not significant (Appendix D reproduces its numbers). A later audit found that its validation stage scored candidates on the optimizer's own training examples and that its loop never scored the unrefined prompt. An earlier version of this study was submitted elsewhere and was not externally reviewed; the first evaluation's numbers are exploratory and are not used as evidence here. A pilot with Qwen3-1.7B in every role then found no effect, but its seven refinement rounds were not independent attempts, and its statistics, task choice and answer-extraction harness were open to criticism. This paper replaces the pilot with a new study.

We ask four research questions. RQ1: with the refinement loop repaired so that rounds are genuinely distinct attempts, does gated MPIR improve a base optimizer's prompt over many tasks, optimizers and seeds? RQ2: is a failure to improve a property of the candidates or of the coarse validation gate? RQ3: does a more capable refining model change the answer? RQ4: does the rubric judge measure anything related to accuracy? (Terms such as cell and run are defined in Section 4.2.) In brief: with a 1.7B refiner the layer changed the prompt in 1 of 80 runs and won in 0 of 45 cells, mainly because that refiner rarely produces a usable candidate that keeps the answer format (RQ1, RQ2); a much larger refiner obtained strict-metric gains in several cells, but most of them reverse under lenient scoring and a rubric-free rewrite reaches gains of the same kind (RQ3); and the rubric score is only weakly related to accuracy (RQ4).

The contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A repaired, instrumented refinement loop.} Rounds differ by sampling and, in the chained variant, by the history of rejected candidates; every round is logged with its outcome (fallback, echo, duplicate, validated), its validation and test scores and the judge's rubric total.
\item \textbf{A pre-specified, multi-seed, multi-task evaluation.} 45 base prompts (three optimizers, nine tasks, up to three seeds), decision rules fixed in a time-stamped protocol, and a random, documented choice of six tasks to complement the three pilot tasks.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics that make the result interpretable.} Oracle scoring of all candidates, acceptance-rule sensitivity, a serving-noise floor and minimum detectable effect, gated and format-protected controls, strict and lenient answer extraction, a comparison with the uncorrected algorithm and a judge-validity analysis; defects of the pipeline and the serving configuration are catalogued (Appendix C).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Manual Prompting Heuristics}

Prompting steers an LLM through in-context learning without changing its weights \cite{liu2023,brown2020}, and has been described as a form of natural-language programming \cite{reynolds2021}. Non-expert authors struggle because they get no structural feedback and over-fit to the few examples they try \cite{zamfirescupereira2023}. Experienced authors rely on heuristics. Few-shot demonstrations help \cite{brown2020,dong2024}, though Min et al. show that format, label space and input distribution matter more than label correctness \cite{min2022}. Chain-of-thought prompting decomposes multistep problems \cite{wei2022,kojima2022}, and coherent, relevant reasoning steps matter more than length \cite{wang2024,wang2023}. Role prompting assigns a persona to guide reasoning \cite{kong2024}, but its benefit is inconsistent: poorly aligned personas can hurt \cite{kim2024}, and a large study found that adding personas to system prompts does not reliably improve performance \cite{zheng2024b}. Step-back prompting abstracts key principles before solving \cite{zheng2024}; a behavioural benchmark of 35 LLMs found that it fosters model-based behaviour in sequential decision tasks \cite{codaforno2024}, although this is weaker evidence for knowledge-intensive reasoning than the original step-back results. Models attend unevenly to information in long contexts \cite{liu2024,yu2025}, and vendor guidance reports recency effects for some models while reporting no difference for GPT-4-class models \cite{microsoft2025}; whether either matters for the short prompts studied here is an empirical question rather than a premise. Self-consistency, a decoding-time heuristic, samples several reasoning paths and votes \cite{wang2023b};

\subsection{Automatic Prompt Optimization and Meta-Prompting}

APO methods generate, evaluate and select prompts iteratively \cite{ramnath2025}. APE induces instructions from input-output demonstrations \cite{zhou2023}; ProTeGi treats LLM critique as a textual gradient and runs a beam search \cite{pryzant2023}; EvoPrompt applies evolutionary operators \cite{guo2024}; OPRO conditions an LLM on a trajectory of scored prompts \cite{yang2024}; PromptWizard combines critique-based refinement, example selection and self-generated reasoning \cite{agarwal2025}. DSPy, TextGrad, Self-Refine and Reflexion generalize the idea of optimizing or refining text with LLM feedback \cite{khattab2024,yuksekgonul2025,madaan2023,shinn2023}. Meta-prompting supplies an LLM with a template that structures the critique (see \cite{cui2025} for a survey): PE2 uses a step-by-step diagnosis template \cite{ye2024}, dual-phase methods add a meta-instruction-guided initialization \cite{yang2024b}, and PROPEL injects expert-derived principles as priors \cite{mayilvaghanan2025}. Closest in spirit to MPIR, a two-stage approach for few-shot relation extraction first searches and then locally edits the winner \cite{chakma2026}. More recent work explores evolutionary reflection \cite{agrawal2026}, error taxonomies \cite{singh2026}, format optimization \cite{liu2025}, debate-based fitness \cite{nair2025} and RL over edits \cite{lin2025}, and several studies target small or open-weight models: gradient-guided optimization with small models \cite{das2025}, minimal-edit optimization for on-device models \cite{shim2026}, and error-driven optimization of arithmetic reasoning \cite{pandy2026}. Others distill reasoning behaviour into prompts \cite{badhe2026}, regularize prompts for token efficiency \cite{shah2026}, question what drives optimizer gains \cite{zhu2025}, or codify principled instructions \cite{bsharat2023}.

\subsection{Limits of Self-Refinement and of LLM Judges}

MPIR asks a model to critique and rewrite its own prompt, which places it in the self-refinement family \cite{madaan2023,shinn2023}. Evidence on intrinsic self-correction is sobering: LLMs cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without external feedback \cite{huang2024}, and small models need strong verifiers to benefit from correction \cite{zhang2024}. MPIR's critic is also an LLM judge, and LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases \cite{zheng2023,wang2024c,panickssery2024}. Finally, benchmark accuracy depends on prompt format and on how answers are extracted \cite{sclar2024,wang2024d,zheng2024c}, which matters here because MPIR's gate and our metrics both rely on a delimiter-based extraction. These strands predict that a 1.7B model refining its own prompts may fail for reasons unrelated to the rubric, which is why our design varies the refiner, validates the judge and re-scores answers leniently.

\subsection{Reporting Negative Results}

Reproducibility programmes \cite{pineau2021} and guidance on reporting experimental results \cite{dodge2019} call for budgets, variance and full protocols. Variance across seeds and data splits can exceed the effect of a method \cite{bouthillier2021}, significance testing in NLP needs care \cite{dror2018}, and small test sets leave studies under-powered \cite{card2020}. We follow this guidance by fixing decision rules in advance, reporting per-cell paired differences with intervals rather than tests that cannot be informative, and quantifying the noise floor and the minimum detectable effect.

\section{The MPIR Layer Under Test}

MPIR is a three-stage cycle applied to a prompt P0 that an upstream APO method has already produced: (1) a language model scores P0 against a seven-criteria rubric of manual prompting heuristics and recommends improvements; (2) it rewrites P0 accordingly; (3) the rewrite is scored on validation questions and kept only if it beats the incumbent (Figure 1). We describe it here as the object under test, not as a contribution.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let (Qv, Av) = \{(qi, ai)\}, i = 1..M, be a validation set of M question-answer pairs and A(L, P) the accuracy of the target model L on it under prompt P. Let R(P0) be the set of candidate prompts that the refinement loop produces from P0. MPIR returns

Pfinal = argmax over P' in \{P0\} $\cup$ R(P0) of A(L, P'),

with ties resolved in favour of P0 (a candidate must be strictly better). Including P0 in the candidate set is what makes it possible for the procedure to conclude that no refinement helps.

\subsection{Algorithm and Variants}

Algorithm 1 is the repaired loop. It scores P0 first, so the incumbent score Abest starts at A(L, P0) instead of minus infinity. In the original implementation the incumbent score started at minus infinity, so the first round's candidate was accepted whatever it scored, which is the defect Section 5.9 quantifies. Choosing among \{P0\} $\cup$ candidates by held-out score is standard in APO loops, so we regard this as an implementation defect of ours, not as a general property of the method.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{MPIR with a validation gate (repaired)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require L: target LLM; L': refining LLM; Meval, Mrefine: meta-prompts; R: rubric; P0: prompt from an APO run; (Qv, Av): validation set; N: rounds; mode in \{independent, chained\}; $\tau$: sampling temperature
\Ensure Pfinal
\State Pbest $\leftarrow$ P0; Abest $\leftarrow$ Validate(L, P0, (Qv, Av)); H $\leftarrow$ empty list \Comment{score the baseline first}
\For{t = 1 to N}
\State Psrc $\leftarrow$ P0 if mode = independent else Pbest
\State S $\leftarrow$ Evaluate(Meval, L', Psrc, R; $\tau$) \Comment{rubric critique}
\State P' $\leftarrow$ Refine(Mrefine, L', Psrc, S, H; $\tau$) \Comment{H is empty when independent}
\State outcome $\leftarrow$ Classify(P') \Comment{fallback, echo, duplicate or validated}
\If{outcome = validated}
\State A' $\leftarrow$ Validate(L, P', (Qv, Av)); append (P', A') to H
\State if A' > Abest then Pbest $\leftarrow$ P'; Abest $\leftarrow$ A' end if \Comment{strict: a tie keeps the incumbent}
\EndIf
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} Pbest
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The pilot ran what we call the legacy loop: Psrc = P0 in every round, H empty and $\tau$ = 0. Because Evaluate and Refine then receive identical input in every round, the seven rounds were one request repeated, up to serving non-determinism, and 'seven rounds without acceptance' meant roughly one distinct attempt per cell. The repaired loop differs between rounds in two ways: independent mode samples at $\tau$ = 0.7 so each round is a fresh draw, and chained mode refines the current incumbent and shows the model the earlier candidates with their validation scores, as OPRO does with its trajectory \cite{yang2024}. We study seven variants (Table 2). Two of them, V1P and V2P, are format-protected: the answer-format line is appended to every candidate, as it is for the rubric-free rewrite and for the prompts of the base optimizers. They were added after an audit found that the refinement request does not ask the refiner to keep the answer-format line, which the 1.7B refiner then dropped in most candidates (Section 5.2); they are exploratory.

\subsection{The Seven-Criteria Rubric}

The rubric was assembled from the prompting literature \cite{schulhoff2024,sahoo2024}, industry guidance \cite{boonstra2025,anthropic2025,openai2025,microsoft2025} and iterative experimentation on BBH-style tasks. It scores a prompt from 1 to 5 on: (1) Role Prompting, (2) Step-Back Reasoning, (3) Guided Chain of Thought, (4) Instruction and Separation, (5) Output Format with Examples, (6) Worked Reasoning in Examples and (7) Conclusion, for a total out of 35. The evidence behind the criteria is uneven. Chain-of-thought and demonstrations are well supported \cite{wei2022,wang2023,min2022}. Role prompting is the least supported, since its benefit is inconsistent \cite{kim2024,zheng2024b}. The Conclusion criterion extrapolates positional-bias results from long contexts \cite{liu2024,yu2025} to short prompts, and the vendor page we cite for it states that instruction placement made no difference for GPT-4-class models \cite{microsoft2025}. Industry sources are practitioner guidance rather than empirical evidence; they are living documents, so the reference list gives archived copies and access dates. The rubric was refined during the first evaluation using BBH tasks, including the three Tier-1 tasks, so Tier 1 is not independent of the rubric's development; the six random Tier-2 tasks are.

The rubric text used in the pilot was internally inconsistent: it announced 'five defined criteria', asked for 'all 6 criteria' and 'Total Score: X/25' while listing seven criteria. We found this during the audit. The primary variants use a corrected text (seven criteria, X/35); variant V2L keeps the original text as a sensitivity check.

\subsection{Evaluation, Refinement and Output Guards}

The evaluation meta-prompt (Figure 2) asks the refining model to apply the rubric to the prompt and report scores, strengths, weaknesses and 7-10 suggestions. The refinement request (Figure 3) asks for a rewrite wrapped in <START> and <END>; in chained mode it also lists the earlier candidates with their validation accuracy and asks the model not to repeat them. Three output guards exist, and each outcome is logged per round. A response without delimiters is a fallback: it cannot be validated, and the pilot's code returned P0 in that case, which ties the baseline by construction. A candidate containing phrases verbatim from the evaluation template, or APE's induction template, is an echo and is rejected before validation. A candidate textually identical to the incumbent, to P0 or to an earlier candidate is a duplicate. Only the remaining candidates are validated. The refinement request of V1 and V2 does not ask the refiner to keep the answer-format line (the free-rewrite prompt does), and a candidate is scored exactly as written; this asymmetry matters for small refiners and is examined in Section 5.2.

\subsection{The Validation Gate}

A candidate is accepted only if its accuracy on the 25 validation questions strictly exceeds the incumbent's. With 25 questions accuracy moves in steps of 4 percentage points, and one question of serving noise equals a step, so acceptance is rare by design. Whether the gate or the candidates explain the absence of accepted refinements is research question RQ2, which we answer by scoring every candidate on the full test split (the oracle analysis) and by re-applying other acceptance rules offline (Section 5.4).

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Pre-Specification and Decision Rules}

The pilot's go/no-go rule was written in the project's planning document (REBUILD.md, commit 7612aa0, 2026-09-03) before the pilot ran. That is a time-stamped plan in a version-controlled repository, not an entry in a public registry, so we describe the study as pre-specified, not pre-registered; several pipeline fixes were also made while the pilot ran (Appendix C). The chronology is: first evaluation and audit (before September 2026); pilot, 2026-09-04 and 05; first submission to this journal, 2026-09-05; decision and reviews, 2026-10-19; revision protocol committed 2026-10-19 (commit 8fd1397), before any revised experiment; revised experiments, 2026-10-20 and 21. The decision rules below are reproduced from that protocol. Its dated amendments, which include one change of the refiners used for D2 and later exploratory additions, are listed with their dates in Appendix F.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{D1 (RQ1).} Primary outcome per cell: paired difference in test accuracy between the final MPIR prompt and P0 (10,000-resample paired bootstrap, 95\% percentile interval); a cell is a win if the difference is positive and the interval excludes zero. The effect 'reproduces' if at least 15 of 45 cells are wins and the mean cell difference is at least +2.0 points; it is 'null' if at most 2 of 45 are wins and the mean lies within $\pm$1.0 point; otherwise 'mixed'. The protocol did not say how the up to three MPIR seeds of a cell are combined: we pool them by averaging the per-example outcomes of the final prompts over the seeds before the bootstrap. The planned 135 runs (45 cells times three MPIR seeds) were cut to 80 for compute reasons, so cells have one to three runs.
\item \textbf{D2 (RQ3).} Capability dependence is supported if the mean cell difference with the strongest refiner exceeds that with the 1.7B refiner by at least 2.0 points and at least 3 of 9 cells are wins with that refiner. The protocol named a local 8B refiner; because the shared GPU was too slow for the extra servers, the strongest-refiner role was reassigned on 2026-10-20 (before any result with these refiners was inspected) to GPT-6 Luna, with Qwen3-235B-A22B as an intermediate rung. Any statement about Qwen3-235B against D2 is therefore post hoc.
\item \textbf{D3 (RQ2).} The gate is limiting if the best oracle candidate beats P0 on test by at least 3.0 points in at least 3 of 9 cells although the gated final prompt did not.
\end{itemize}

McNemar, GEE and Wilcoxon tests on identical prompts cannot be informative, and the Wilcoxon test on three tasks cannot go below p = 0.25, so none is reported as inference. Rubric-accuracy correlations, lenient parsing, the uncorrected-algorithm comparison, the noise floor and the minimum detectable effect are descriptive.

\subsection{Data, Splits and Task Selection}

BBH has 23 tasks in 27 task files, about 6,511 examples \cite{suzgun2023}. Each task is split three ways with random.Random(seed).sample over the original example order: 25 optimizer-training examples, 25 validation examples for the gate, and the remainder as test (200 examples for every task used here except penguins\_\allowbreak{}in\_\allowbreak{}a\_\allowbreak{}table, which has 96). The partition is stored with the original example indices, is shared by all optimizers and conditions for a given (task, seed), and no stage touches test data during optimization or refinement. Tier 1 is the three pilot tasks (hyperbaton, ruin\_\allowbreak{}names, penguins\_\allowbreak{}in\_\allowbreak{}a\_\allowbreak{}table) at seeds 42, 43 and 44. They were chosen because the leaked first evaluation showed the largest gains there, so they are a biased sample and may be subject to winner's-curse effects. Tier 2 is six tasks drawn uniformly at random, without replacement, from the other 24 task files with random.Random(20261019): temporal\_\allowbreak{}sequences, movie\_\allowbreak{}recommendation, disambiguation\_\allowbreak{}qa, geometric\_\allowbreak{}shapes, salient\_\allowbreak{}translation\_\allowbreak{}error\_\allowbreak{}detection and sports\_\allowbreak{}understanding, at seed 42. In total there are 45 cells, each with its own base prompt P0.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cell.} A (task, optimizer, split seed) combination with its own split and its own base prompt P0.
\item \textbf{Run.} One execution of a refinement variant on a cell with one MPIR seed (the seed of the refiner's sampling); a cell has one to three runs.
\item \textbf{Variant, round.} A refinement procedure (V0 to V3, V1P, V2P, V2L; Table 2); one round is one evaluate-refine-validate step, with N = 7 rounds per run.
\item \textbf{Split seed, MPIR seed.} The seed that draws the train, validation and test split and the optimizer's own randomness (42, 43, 44), and the seed of the refiner's sampling (1 to 3).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Models and Serving}

The target model is Qwen3-1.7B \cite{qwenteam2025} with thinking disabled and greedy decoding, at most 1024 new tokens. The refining model is the same model in the main grid. In the capability experiment the refiner is Qwen3-235B-A22B (the 2507 instruct release, a mixture-of-experts model from the target's family) or GPT-6 Luna \cite{luna2026}, both reached through the OpenRouter API on 2026-10-20 and 21 with temperature 0.7, a 3000-token limit and, for GPT-6 Luna, minimal reasoning effort. Meta-prompt calls allow 1500 (evaluation) and 3000 (refinement) new tokens. The target and the 1.7B refiner are served with vLLM \cite{kwon2023} 0.28.0 under WSL2 on one 16 GB RTX 5000 Ada GPU, bf16 weights, a maximum context of 8192 tokens (Appendix C). Greedy decoding is not bit-reproducible under continuous batching, so we measure the resulting noise (Section 5.7).

\subsection{Base Optimizers and Conditions}

APE and ProTeGi are clean-room implementations of the published algorithms \cite{zhou2023,pryzant2023}, run with few\_\allowbreak{}shot\_\allowbreak{}count = 0, hence exemplar-free; ProTeGi scores every successor on a full minibatch instead of using the original bandit scheme. PromptWizard \cite{agarwal2025} is run with synthetic-example generation disabled; its three in-context examples are drawn from the 25 training examples with model-generated reasoning, and its generated expert identity is not used at evaluation. Hyperparameters are in Table 3. Many of the APE and ProTeGi base prompts are not optimized in any strong sense: for two of the three pilot tasks they are the seed instruction "Let's think step by step." plus the answer-format line, and the APE prompt for hyperbaton is its own induction template (Appendix A). PromptWizard is a reduced configuration, because synthetic-example generation was disabled. The base prompts are therefore a mix of weak and degenerate starting points, which limits what refining them can show. Only the expert few-shot condition uses the three BBH-author exemplars \cite{suzgun2023}; the first manuscript's statement that APE and ProTeGi also used them was wrong. Reference conditions are zero-shot CoT \cite{kojima2022} and expert few-shot CoT. The free-rewrite control (Appendix A.3) rewrites P0 with no rubric; V3 adds the gate.

\subsection{Metrics}

Accuracy is the share of test examples whose delimited answer equals the gold answer after removing parentheses, whitespace and case (strict extraction, as in the pilot). Because strict extraction penalizes correct reasoning that is wrapped badly, we add a lenient accuracy computed offline from the saved raw outputs: the response counts as correct if the delimited span (or, if there is none, the last 300 characters) names exactly one option and it is the gold one. Both are reported; strict extraction remains primary because MPIR's gate uses it.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

For every cell we report the paired difference in test accuracy between the final MPIR prompt and P0 with a 95\% paired-bootstrap interval over test examples. Cells with identical prompts have a difference of zero by construction, apart from serving noise. Across cells we report counts of wins, the mean difference and a hierarchical bootstrap interval that resamples tasks and then the cells within each selected task. The noise floor is the distribution of accuracy differences between repeated scorings of the same prompt, and the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power and $\alpha$ = 0.05 follows from the paired discordance rate \cite{dror2018,card2020}. Intervals use the percentile bootstrap \cite{efron1979} and, for single accuracies, the Wilson interval \cite{wilson1927}.

\subsection{Harness Defects and Deviations}

Running against a live model exposed defects that mocked tests could not (truncation of long responses, answer normalization, delimiter mismatches in expert exemplars, template mismatches between an optimizer's own and MPIR's evaluation harness, two echo patterns and the unscored baseline). All are described with their magnitude in Appendix C and were fixed before the numbers reported here were produced.

For the 1.7B refiner, 60\% of the chained rounds (337 of 560) never reached validation because the response had no delimiters or echoed a template; of the 193 validated candidates 126 scored below the baseline, 66 tied and 1 scored higher. \subsection{The Repaired Layer on 45 Cells (RQ1, D1)}

D1 was specified and run for the unprotected V2, and Section 5.2 shows that this procedure is limited by the 1.7B refiner's inability to produce usable, format-preserving candidates; the null is therefore a statement about this procedure with this refiner, and not about rubric-guided refinement in general. \subsection{Is the Gate the Bottleneck? (RQ2, D3)}

To separate the candidates from the gate, every validated candidate of MPIR seed 1 was also scored on the full test split. Ungated, the mean test-accuracy change of a 1.7B-refiner candidate relative to its P0 is -27.0 points for the independent variant (41 candidates) and -15.2 points for the chained variant (22), and no cell has a candidate that beats P0 by 3 points or more (0 and 0 of 9 cells); these candidates mostly lack the answer-format line (Section 5.2), so the figure largely reflects format loss. Candidates that keep the format are still not better on average: -24.9 points for the format-protected 1.7B candidates of V1P and V2P (20 candidates, none beating P0 by 3 points or more), -8.8 points for the 1.7B rewrite (33 candidates) and -4.0 points for the Qwen3-235B candidates (58; best candidate per cell +4.0 points on average, 4 cells at +3 or more). D3 requires the best candidate to beat P0 by 3 points in at least 3 of 9 cells while the gated final prompt does not; it is not met for the 1.7B refiner. Validation accuracy tracks test accuracy across candidates (Spearman 0.92 over 275 oracle-scored candidates of all refiners), but this is driven by candidates that score zero; among 1.7B-refiner candidates with positive validation accuracy it is 0.37 (n = 27). The gate can therefore rank broken candidates but is a weaker guide among plausible ones, and a 25-question gate is noisy (Section 5.7). \subsection{Free Rewriting Versus the Rubric}

In the pilot, the ungated rubric-free rewrite of the PromptWizard prompt changed strict accuracy by +2.0, -19.0 and -16.7 points on hyperbaton, ruin\_\allowbreak{}names and penguins\_\allowbreak{}in\_\allowbreak{}a\_\allowbreak{}table (Tables 4 and 5); the first is 4 test items, within the serving noise of Section 5.7, and the others are large losses, so the ungated rewrite was harmful on two of three tasks. The gated rewrite (V3) accepted a rewrite in 16 of 27 runs, against 2 of 27 for the independent rubric variant and 0 of 27 for the chained one (Tier-1 runs). Part of this gap is the format asymmetry of Section 5.2, not the rewriting style. V3 has 2 winning cells (hyperbaton / APE +17.0, ruin\_\allowbreak{}names / ProTeGi +2.8) and 1 cells in which the gate accepted a harmful rewrite (hyperbaton / PromptWizard -9.3), for a mean difference of +0.7 points. So the gate does not reliably protect against harm either, since 25 questions are noisy. We cannot say that unstructured rewriting is worse than rubric-guided refinement: the rubric-free variant did at least as well (+0.7 against -0.5 and +0.0 points), and the rubric adds nothing detectable with this refiner.

One cell out of nine, on a base prompt that opens with an irrelevant sentence (Appendix A), is a gain that a rubric-based rewrite can plausibly claim; there is no multiplicity correction and no replication on another task. The two strong refiners differ, one belongs to the target's family and one was run with minimal reasoning, so capability, family and reasoning budget are confounded, and the experiment is an upper-bound probe, not a controlled capability scale.

\subsubsection{Follow-Up Runs (Exploratory)}

Because the strong-refiner signal appeared after the pre-specified analysis, two follow-up runs were added and are exploratory (Appendix F). First, the rubric-free gated rewrite (V3) with the same Qwen3-235B refiner gives a mean strict difference of +6.0 points with 2 winning cells of 9 (ruin\_\allowbreak{}names / ProTeGi +26.0, hyperbaton / PromptWizard +25.5), against +8.7 points and 4 wins for the rubric-guided variant, and a lenient mean of -0.4 points. A refiner that does not use the rubric therefore reaches gains of the same kind, so the rubric is not what produces them. Second, a second MPIR seed of the rubric-guided variant gives +12.3 points and 4 winning cells, so the strict pattern is stable across seeds.

\subsection{Serving Noise and Minimum Detectable Effect}

Each of the nine pilot base prompts was scored five times on the test and validation splits (identical prompt, greedy decoding). Test accuracy varies with a standard deviation of 1.4 points and a range of 3.3 points per prompt on average; between two scorings of the same prompt 6.5\% of examples change outcome on average (up to 16.6\%), and the pilot and present scorings of the same prompt differ by up to 7.0 points. This is larger than the 2\% assumed in the first submission and is the reason a 25-question validation split is noisy. For 200 test examples the minimum detectable paired effect at 80\% power and $\alpha$ = 0.05 is 5.0 points if only serving noise separates two prompts (paired discordance 6.5\%), and 6.3 to 8.9 points if two different prompts disagree on 10-20\% of examples, as candidates here do. The strict gains of the strong refiner (+30, +29, +12, +11 points) are above this floor; effects of a few points are not detectable at these test sizes.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the Evidence Supports}

With Qwen3-1.7B as target and refiner, the rubric-guided layer, as specified, did not improve the prompts of any of three optimizers on any of nine tasks: the final prompt equalled P0 in 79 of 80 runs, and by the pre-specified rule the outcome is null (Section 5.3). The refinement rounds were made distinct by sampling and history, the failure is not caused by the inconsistent rubric text, and a rubric-free gated rewrite does at least as well (Section 5.5). The main reason is the refiner: most rounds never produce a usable candidate, and the unprotected candidates lose the answer-format line, so under strict extraction they score zero. With the format line appended the refiner still reaches validation in a minority of rounds and 5 of 36 validated candidates beat the baseline (Section 5.2). The null is therefore a result about a 1.7B model's ability to follow a long refinement instruction, and it says little about rubric-guided refinement by a capable model.

The evidence on a more capable refiner is mixed and exploratory. A much larger open-weight refiner produced significant strict gains in four cells in two MPIR seeds, and a strong closed refiner in one. Most of the strict gains are, however, repairs of the answer tag on base prompts that reason about as well as before; under lenient extraction they reverse, because the long structured rewrite makes the 1.7B target reason worse, and a rubric-free gated rewrite by the same refiner reaches gains of the same kind. The one gain that survives both metrics, hyperbaton with PromptWizard, is on a base prompt that opens with an irrelevant sentence, and it is a single cell. So the data do not show that the seven-criteria rubric helps; criterion-level ablations were not run, and the original GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-turbo configuration was not repeated, so the first evaluation's per-task gains remain unexplained and should not be cited as evidence for MPIR.

A second lesson is about measurement. Answer extraction decides what the gate rewards: a gate that uses strict extraction accepts rewrites that fix the tag even when they harm reasoning, and exemplar-free baselines look far weaker than they are. Reporting a lenient metric next to the strict one exposed both effects.

\subsection{Practical Guidance, Scoped to What Was Tested}

The following points follow from these experiments, with Qwen3 models, nine BBH tasks and the optimizers listed; they are not general rules. (i) Before adding a refinement layer, inspect how answers are extracted and check a lenient metric: one worked example of the answer format moved strict accuracy of exemplar-free prompts by +19 and +42 points (APE, ProTeGi), and +2 and +10 points under lenient extraction. (ii) Do not accept a model's rewrite of a prompt without scoring the original prompt as a baseline; with a 1.7B refiner the uncorrected loop lowered accuracy by about 14 points on average in our nine cells (one MPIR seed). (iii) Gate on held-out data, but note that identical prompts scored twice differ on 6.5\% of examples, so differences of a few points on a few hundred questions are noise. (iv) When the refining model is as small as the target, expect most rewrites to be unusable (echoes, missing delimiters, a dropped answer format) and monitor that rate; protect the answer-format line explicitly.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\subsubsection{Internal Validity}

The serving stack is not deterministic: repeated scoring of one prompt spans 3.3 points on average (standard deviation 1.4), which the gate and the minimum detectable effect (5.0 points) take into account but cannot remove. The inference server was restarted several times and its memory settings were changed during the study; cached scores and generations were kept across restarts, so a few interrupted runs combine scorings taken under different settings, with differences of the order of the measured noise. The main grid has 80 runs instead of the planned 135, oracle scoring covers MPIR seed 1 only, the strong-refiner runs use one MPIR seed (a second for one refiner), and the format-protected variants cover only part of the nine Tier-1 seed-42 cells (Section 5.2). Two audit findings, the format asymmetry of V1 and V2 and a lenient parser that rejected bare letters, were found late and corrected after the main grid had run; the main grid was not re-run with protection. The follow-up runs and the protected variants were added after results were seen. The APE prompt for hyperbaton is a degenerate artifact of our implementation (Appendix A), and many APE and ProTeGi base prompts are weak, which limits what refining them can show.

\subsubsection{External Validity}

One target model family and size was used in every run. Tier 1 was chosen from a biased statistic and its tasks were also used while the rubric was developed; Tier 2 is a random draw of six tasks from 24, with one split seed. The strong-refiner experiment covers nine cells. The original closed-model configuration, including GPT-4o as refiner, was not rerun; GPT-6 Luna is a different model and not a replication of it.

\subsubsection{Construct and Conclusion Validity}

Strict answer extraction partly measures output formatting, and the lenient metric is an approximation. The rubric and its judge are weakly related to accuracy (Section 5.8), no human ratings were collected, and the rubric's criteria were not ablated. The statistics are descriptive by design: with identical prompts in most cells, tests of differences would be uninformative, the nine pilot cells share three tasks and three optimizers and are not independent, and the cell-level wins of the strong refiners carry no multiplicity correction.

\section{Conclusion}

We re-tested a rubric-guided refinement layer for automatically optimized prompts after repairing its refinement loop and fixing the decision rules in advance. With a 1.7B model refining its own prompts the layer produced no improvement in 45 cells (one of 80 runs accepted a candidate), mainly because that refiner rarely produces a usable, format-preserving candidate. A much larger refiner obtained strict-metric gains in several cells, but most reverse under lenient scoring and a rubric-free rewrite reaches gains of the same kind, so the rubric is not shown to be what helps; one gain, on a single task, holds under both metrics. With a 1.7B refiner the uncorrected loop would have reduced accuracy by about 14 points (its effect with the original GPT-4o refiner is unknown), strict answer extraction accounts for much of the apparent weakness of exemplar-free baselines, and identical prompts do not score identically on this serving stack. These conclusions are limited to Qwen3-based targets, nine tasks and the optimizers tested, and the first evaluation's GPT-4o results remain unverified. Code, per-round logs and generations are public.

\section{Protocol and Deviations}

The decision rules of Section 4.1 were fixed in REVISION\_\allowbreak{}PROTOCOL.md before any revised experiment ran (commit 8fd1397, 2026-10-19). The amendments below are recorded in that file; none is an entry in a registry. Amendments 1 to 7 were made before the results of the affected experiment were inspected; amendments 8 and 9 were made after results were seen and are exploratory; V1P and V2P were stopped before all nine cells finished.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1. 2026-10-20, compute.} Serving stack vLLM 0.28.0 under WSL2 with measured throughput of about 1.2k tokens/s; oracle test scoring of every validated candidate for MPIR seed 1 only; one validation score per candidate in the main grid G1; V2L with one seed and no oracle scoring.
\item \textbf{2. 2026-10-20, sampling.} Refiner sampling uses temperature 0.7 without top-p (the protocol text said top-p 0.95), because top-p was about three times slower on this stack.
\item \textbf{3. 2026-10-20, grouping.} Tier-1 seed-42 cells are run only in the mechanism group G2, which contains the V2 runs with oracle scoring; G1 covers the other 36 cells.
\item \textbf{4. 2026-10-20, refiners for D2.} Local 4B and 8B refiners were not run; the strongest-refiner role of D2 went to GPT-6 Luna and Qwen3-235B-A22B through OpenRouter (Section 4.1).
\item \textbf{5. 2026-10-20 and 21, scale.} The main grid has 80 runs instead of the planned 135; MPIR seeds beyond 1 were cut after seed 1 was complete.
\item \textbf{6. 2026-10-20, pooling.} Cell-level differences pool the MPIR seeds of a cell by averaging per-example outcomes (Section 4.1).
\item \textbf{7. 2026-10-20, optimizer implementations.} No change to APE, ProTeGi or PromptWizard code or hyperparameters between the pilot and the extra seeds and tasks.
\item \textbf{8. 2026-10-21, exploratory.} After the Qwen3-235B refiner showed strict-metric gains, the rubric-free gated rewrite with that refiner and a second MPIR seed were added (Section 5.6.1).
\item \textbf{9. 2026-10-21, exploratory.} After an audit found that V1 and V2 candidates are scored without the answer-format line, the format-protected variants V1P and V2P were added, and the lenient parser was corrected to accept everything the strict parser accepts (Section 5.2).
\end{itemize}
